# Financial Markets – Session 5: Monetary Policy, Money & Central Bank Balance Sheets

## 1. Session Overview

Session 5 shifted from fixed-income mechanics toward the broader **monetary system**.

The class connected current financial developments with a historical and institutional question:

> **Where does money come from, who controls it, and why do interest rates matter so much?**

The session developed five major themes:

1. global liquidity and the competition for capital;
2. fiscal policy versus monetary policy;
3. the historical evolution of the international monetary system;
4. the functions and different forms of money;
5. central-bank balance sheets and monetary aggregates.

A recurring idea throughout the session was that financial systems depend fundamentally on:

> **confidence**

Money works because people trust that it will continue to be accepted and retain enough value to perform its economic functions.

---

# 2. Current Context: Where Is Global Liquidity Going?

The class returned to a theme developed in previous sessions:

> **There is a limited pool of global savings, while financing needs are increasing.**

Several competing demands for capital were discussed.

### Governments

Governments need enormous amounts of financing when:

$$Public\ Spending > Tax\ Revenue$$

The resulting deficit must normally be financed through debt issuance.

### Artificial Intelligence

AI infrastructure requires very large amounts of capital expenditure:

* data centres;
* chips;
* energy infrastructure;
* computing capacity;
* networks.

The class therefore returned to the idea that:

$$Government\ Borrowing \quad vs. \quad AI\ Investment$$

are increasingly competing for the same global capital.

---

# 3. Japan and the Carry Trade

The **carry trade** was revisited.

The basic mechanism is simple.

Suppose an investor can:

1. borrow in a currency with very low interest rates;
2. convert the borrowed money into another currency;
3. invest in an asset offering a higher return.

Conceptually:

$$Borrow\ Cheap \rightarrow Convert\ Currency \rightarrow Invest\ at\ Higher\ Yield$$

Japan has historically been particularly important because Japanese interest rates remained extremely low for many years.

This made the yen an important funding currency.

However, the strategy contains significant risks.

---

# 4. Carry Trade Risk

The apparent return difference is not free money.

The investor faces at least two important risks:

### Interest-rate risk

The cost of borrowing may increase.

### Exchange-rate risk

The funding currency may appreciate against the investment currency.

A carry trade can therefore become unprofitable even if the yield on the target investment remains unchanged.

The broader lesson is:

> **Whenever an investment appears to generate a return difference, identify the risk that makes that return possible.**

---

# 5. Changing Sources of Global Capital

The class discussed how some traditional sources of global liquidity may be changing.

Historically, substantial capital has flowed internationally from:

* Japan;
* energy-exporting economies;
* sovereign investors;
* institutional savings.

If these economies begin to retain more capital domestically, the amount available to finance other economies decreases.

At the same time, financing needs elsewhere may continue to rise.

This creates potential pressure on:

* yields;
* government borrowing costs;
* corporate financing;
* asset valuations.

---

# 6. Fiscal Policy vs Monetary Policy

A key conceptual distinction was introduced.

## Fiscal Policy

Fiscal policy concerns:

* **taxes**;
* **government expenditure**.

It is fundamentally controlled by governments and legislatures.

Conceptually:

$$Fiscal\ Policy = Taxes + Public\ Spending$$

When expenditure exceeds revenues:

$$Deficit = Spending-Taxes$$

and the government normally needs financing.

---

## Monetary Policy

Monetary policy concerns the monetary and financial conditions of the economy.

Central banks can influence these conditions through instruments such as:

* interest rates;
* liquidity provision;
* asset purchases;
* reserve policies;
* foreign-exchange intervention.

This session concentrated primarily on **monetary policy**.

---

# 7. Why History Matters

The class then used history to explain why modern monetary institutions exist.

The objective was not to study every historical detail.

Instead, the professor emphasized a recurring principle:

> **Those who do not know history are doomed to repeat it.**

Modern central banking, monetary policy and international financial institutions emerged from historical experiences with:

* wars;
* inflation;
* hyperinflation;
* exchange-rate instability;
* financial crises.

---

# 8. German Hyperinflation

The German hyperinflation of the early 1920s was discussed as an extreme example of monetary instability.

Following World War I, Germany faced substantial financial obligations.

The class emphasized the connection between:

* fiscal pressures;
* money creation;
* rapidly increasing prices;
* collapse of confidence.

Hyperinflation is not simply “prices going up quickly.”

It changes how society functions.

When money loses value extremely rapidly, individuals begin dedicating enormous amounts of time to protecting themselves from the monetary system itself.

---

# 9. Inflation as a Problem of Confidence

The standard definition of inflation concerns a sustained increase in the general price level.

The class added another interpretation:

> **Inflation can also be understood as declining confidence in the monetary system.**

The more rapidly money loses purchasing power, the less attractive it becomes as a store of value.

In extreme cases, people try to exchange money immediately for:

* goods;
* foreign currencies;
* real assets;
* anything expected to retain value better.

This leads to a broader institutional idea:

$$Stable\ Money \leftrightarrow Trust$$

---

# 10. The Social Cost of Hyperinflation

Hyperinflation creates costs far beyond finance.

Imagine an economy where prices change dramatically from one day to the next.

Households must devote time to:

* finding goods;
* protecting savings;
* exchanging currencies;
* deciding when to buy;
* avoiding losses caused simply by holding money.

Businesses face similar problems.

The financial system therefore generates enormous **noise**.

Resources that should be dedicated to productive activity become dedicated to managing monetary instability.

This is one reason monetary stability matters economically and socially.

---

# 11. Bretton Woods

The class then moved to the post-World War II monetary architecture.

In **1944**, representatives of different countries met at Bretton Woods to design a more stable international financial system.

The system involved fixed exchange-rate relationships centred on the U.S. dollar.

The dollar itself maintained a fixed relationship with gold.

The class used the historical parity:

$$\$35 \approx 1\ ounce\ of\ gold$$

as the key reference point.

Conceptually:

$$Gold \leftrightarrow US\ Dollar \leftrightarrow Other\ Currencies$$

---

# 12. Building the Post-War Financial Order

The period surrounding the end of World War II was presented as a moment in which much of the modern international architecture emerged.

Institutions and arrangements created during this era included elements of:

* the international monetary system;
* international economic cooperation;
* global governance.

The central idea was that the post-war world attempted to build institutions capable of avoiding some of the instability experienced during the interwar period.

---

# 13. Why Bretton Woods Came Under Pressure

Maintaining a fixed monetary system becomes difficult when domestic policy requires large amounts of expenditure.

The class linked the eventual breakdown of the Bretton Woods system with major U.S. financial pressures during the 1960s and early 1970s, including:

* international military expenditure;
* the Vietnam War;
* other large public programmes.

If the quantity of dollars expands while the quantity of gold supporting the system does not expand proportionally, confidence in the fixed convertibility mechanism becomes increasingly difficult to maintain.

---

# 14. The End of Bretton Woods

The class discussed the suspension of dollar-gold convertibility under President Richard Nixon.

This represented a major structural change.

Before:

$$Dollar \rightarrow Fixed\ Gold\ Relationship$$

After:

$$Dollar \rightarrow Fiat\ Monetary\ System$$

The value of modern money increasingly depended not on convertibility into gold but on:

* institutions;
* central banks;
* government credibility;
* economic capacity;
* confidence.

---

# 15. Trust Becomes Central

Once money is not mechanically convertible into a commodity such as gold, confidence becomes even more important.

Why does someone accept a dollar, euro or yen?

Because they believe that:

1. other people will also accept it;
2. the monetary system will continue functioning;
3. its value will remain sufficiently stable.

This brings the discussion back to the concept of trust.

> **Modern money is ultimately an institutional promise.**

---

# 16. The 1970s: Energy and Inflation

The breakdown of Bretton Woods was followed by a period of substantial monetary and economic instability.

The class linked the 1970s with:

* energy shocks;
* oil;
* inflation;
* changes in the international monetary order.

Norway was mentioned in this context because of the development of its petroleum resources and the later importance of its sovereign wealth.

The broader financial lesson was again that:

> monetary systems, energy markets, geopolitics and capital flows cannot be studied completely independently.

---

# 17. European Monetary Integration

The instability of the international monetary environment also contributed to European efforts to create greater monetary coordination.

Europe increasingly sought a system that would reduce excessive dependence on the U.S. dollar and limit exchange-rate instability within Europe.

This historical process eventually contributed to the development of the European monetary architecture studied today.

---

# 18. What Is Money?

The class then asked a deceptively simple question:

> **What is money?**

Money is difficult to define by describing its physical form.

Money can be:

* paper;
* coins;
* bank deposits;
* digital records.

A more useful definition is therefore based on what money **does**.

---

# 19. The Three Functions of Money

Money traditionally performs three basic functions.

## 1. Medium of Exchange

Money allows goods and services to be traded without direct barter.

Instead of requiring:

$$\text{Good A} \leftrightarrow \text{Good B}$$

we can use:

$$\text{Good A} \rightarrow \text{Money} \rightarrow \text{Good B}$$

---

## 2. Unit of Account

Money provides a common language for measuring economic value.

It allows us to compare:

* a coffee;
* a house;
* a salary;
* a company;
* a bond.

Without a common unit of account, economic calculation becomes substantially more difficult.

---

## 3. Store of Value

Money allows purchasing power to be transferred through time.

You do not need to spend all your income immediately.

You can hold money for future use.

However, inflation weakens this function.

$$\text{Inflation}\uparrow \;\Rightarrow\; \text{Real Value of Money}\downarrow$$

---

# 20. Money and Liquidity Are Not the Same Thing

The class distinguished between **money** and **liquid assets**.

A publicly traded stock may be highly liquid.

It can often be converted into money quickly.

But that does not automatically make the stock itself money.

This distinction will become important when discussing monetary aggregates.

There is a continuum:

$$\text{Cash} \rightarrow \text{Bank Deposits} \rightarrow \text{Highly Liquid Assets} \rightarrow \text{Less Liquid Assets}$$

The boundaries depend on the definition being used.

---

# 21. How Much Money Exists?

The question “How much money exists in the world?” does not have one simple answer.

It depends on what we define as money.

Possible categories include:

* physical currency;
* central-bank money;
* bank deposits;
* broader monetary aggregates;
* liquid financial assets.

This is why economists use different **monetary aggregates**.

The class previewed measures such as:

* monetary base;
* narrow money;
* broader money.

These will be developed further in the next session.

---

# 22. The Central Bank Balance Sheet

A central bank, like any other financial institution, has a balance sheet:

$$Assets = Liabilities + Equity$$

Understanding the balance sheet helps explain where central-bank money comes from.

---

# 23. Central Bank Liabilities

One of the most important concepts introduced was that **money issued by a central bank appears on the liability side of its balance sheet**.

Central-bank liabilities may include:

* physical currency in circulation;
* reserves or deposits held by commercial banks at the central bank.

Therefore, money is not limited to banknotes and coins.

A substantial part of central-bank money exists electronically.

This is the foundation of the **monetary base**.

---

# 24. Monetary Base

The monetary base consists principally of central-bank money:

$$Monetary\ Base \approx Currency + Bank\ Reserves$$

It can therefore be understood through the liability side of the central-bank balance sheet.

This is different from broader measures of money that include deposits created within the commercial banking system.

---

# 25. Central Bank Assets

What appears on the other side?

Central-bank assets may include:

* gold;
* government bonds;
* foreign-currency assets;
* loans;
* other securities.

This means central-bank actions can fundamentally change the composition and size of their balance sheets.

For example:

$$\text{Central Bank buys Government Bond}$$

can correspond to:

$$\text{Central Bank Assets}\uparrow$$

and simultaneously:

$$\text{Central Bank Money}\uparrow$$

depending on how the transaction is implemented.

---

# 26. Government Debt on Central-Bank Balance Sheets

The class highlighted the large-scale purchase of government debt by central banks following major financial crises.

This creates an important interaction between:

* monetary policy;
* sovereign debt markets;
* financial stability.

It also raises an institutional question that will return later:

> **Where should the boundary between fiscal policy and monetary policy be?**

The class emphasized that these two functions are conceptually different even when crises make their interaction increasingly important.

---

# 27. Central Banks Can Hold More Than Government Bonds

The example of the **Swiss National Bank** was used to illustrate how unusual central-bank balance sheets can become.

A central bank may hold:

* gold;
* government bonds;
* foreign currencies;
* other financial securities.

The class discussed how the Swiss central bank has also held a substantial portfolio of listed equities.

This makes its balance sheet appear, in some respects, closer to that of a large institutional investor.

The important principle is:

> **Always look at the actual balance sheet rather than assuming every central bank operates identically.**

---

# 28. Central Bank Objectives

The class distinguished between different central-bank mandates.

A central bank typically places substantial weight on:

> **price stability**

The European Central Bank was presented primarily through its inflation objective.

The Federal Reserve was discussed in relation to a broader mandate involving both:

* price stability;
* labour-market conditions.

Different institutional mandates can therefore produce different policy considerations.

---

# 29. Central Bank Tools

Several possible central-bank tools were introduced.

### Interest rates

The main tool developed in the course.

Changing interest rates affects:

* borrowing;
* saving;
* consumption;
* investment;
* bond prices;
* financial conditions.

### Balance-sheet operations

Central banks can buy or sell assets.

### Foreign-exchange intervention

Particularly relevant for smaller economies.

A central bank may buy or sell its own currency or foreign currencies in an attempt to influence:

* exchange rates;
* monetary conditions;
* inflation.

---

# 31. A Brief AI Methodology Aside

Although not the main subject of Session 5, the class returned briefly to the use of AI.

The central message was that there is no single universal “AI tool” for every task.

LLMs are particularly powerful for:

* analysing information;
* structuring knowledge;
* learning;
* generating text;
* working with transcriptions.

But they are not necessarily the most efficient tool for every formatting or production task.

The principle was:

> **Use the correct tool for the correct layer of the problem.**

For example, a presentation workflow might be:

$$\text{Knowledge} \rightarrow \text{Structured Markdown} \rightarrow \text{Slide Design} \rightarrow \text{Automated Production}$$

rather than asking one LLM to handle every step simultaneously.

---

# 32. Thinking in Orders of Magnitude

The class also emphasized the importance of developing numerical reference points.

Financial news regularly uses numbers such as:

* billions;
* trillions;
* GDP;
* public debt;
* market capitalization.

Without reference points, these figures become almost meaningless.

Students should therefore build an approximate mental map of:

* global GDP;
* U.S. GDP;
* major national economies;
* major stock-market values;
* sovereign debt.

The exact number changes.

The **order of magnitude** is what allows financial information to be interpreted quickly.

---

# 33. Global Debt

The comparison between:

* global GDP;
* global money;
* global debt;
* real estate;
* stock markets;
* derivatives

was used to emphasize the extraordinary scale of the modern financial system.

The professor highlighted global debt as a particularly important structural issue.

Debt allows economic agents to bring future resources into the present.

But:

$$\text{Debt Today} \rightarrow \text{Future Payment Obligation}$$

Large amounts of debt therefore represent claims on future income.

The question ultimately becomes:

> **Who will generate the future cash flows required to service today's debt?**

### 1. Global capital is scarce.

Governments, companies and AI investment all compete for financing.

---

### 2. Fiscal policy and monetary policy are different.

$$Fiscal\ Policy = Taxes + Spending$$

while monetary policy concerns money, liquidity and financial conditions.

---

### 3. Money ultimately depends on confidence.

Modern fiat currencies work because society trusts the monetary system.

---

### 4. Central-bank money is a liability of the central bank.

$$Monetary\ Base \approx Currency + Bank\ Reserves$$

This is crucial for understanding how money is created and how monetary policy works.

---

### 5. Interest rates connect monetary policy with financial markets.

Changes in rates affect:

* bonds;
* credit;
* investment;
* consumption;
* currencies;
* valuations.

---

# Final Takeaway

Session 5 moved from **bonds** to the system that ultimately determines many of the variables used to price those bonds.

Behind interest rates sits monetary policy.

Behind monetary policy sits the central bank.

Behind the central bank sits a balance sheet.

And behind modern money itself sits something less tangible but absolutely fundamental:

> **trust.**

Understanding financial markets therefore requires understanding not only how assets are priced, but also **how money is created, why people accept it, and how monetary institutions preserve confidence in it.**
