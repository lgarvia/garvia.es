# Financial Markets – Session 3: Fixed Income, Bond Pricing & Market Liquidity

## 1. Session Overview

Session 3 moved decisively into **fixed income**.

The class connected current macro-financial developments with the mechanics of bond valuation. The central idea was that governments, companies and large technological investments all compete for the same limited pool of capital.

From there, the session developed the basic structure of bond markets:

* primary versus secondary markets;
* face value;
* maturity;
* yield;
* zero-coupon bonds;
* coupon bonds;
* bond pricing through discounted cash flows;
* premium, par and discount bonds;
* holding-period return;
* the inverse relationship between rates and bond prices;
* the intuition behind duration.

The financial logic developed in Session 2 — **discounting future cash flows** — was therefore applied directly to bonds.

---

# 2. Starting from the Real World

The class began, as usual, by looking at what is happening in markets and the broader economy.

The professor emphasized the need to distinguish between:

* **noise**, and
* **financially relevant information**.

Current events matter when they affect:

* expected cash flows;
* interest rates;
* risk;
* liquidity;
* government borrowing;
* capital allocation.

The objective is not to predict the future perfectly.

It is to collect enough information to think critically about the possible outcomes.

> **Finance is not about knowing the future. It is about managing an uncertain future with the information available.**

---

# 3. Central Banks and Global Capital Flows

The Federal Reserve and the Bank of Japan were highlighted as particularly important.

Japan remains central to the course because of its historical role as a source of extremely cheap financing.

For many years:

$$Low\ Japanese\ Rates \rightarrow Cheap\ Yen\ Funding \rightarrow Capital\ Invested\ Elsewhere$$

As Japanese yields rise, however, incentives begin to change.

Capital that previously moved out of Japan may become more inclined to remain in Japan or return to Japanese assets.

This is important because:

> **changes in Japanese rates can alter global capital flows.**

The class linked this to the carry trade discussed in previous sessions.

---

# 4. Governments Need Money

A major theme of the session was the growing demand for financing.

Governments finance themselves primarily through:

1. taxes;
2. borrowing.

If:

$$Government\ Spending > Tax\ Revenue$$

the difference must normally be financed by issuing debt.

Therefore:

$$Deficit \rightarrow Borrowing \rightarrow Government\ Bond\ Issuance$$

This means governments become major competitors for available liquidity.

The United States, France, Spain and other countries were mentioned as examples of governments facing substantial financing needs.

---

# 5. Where Does the Money Come From?

The session then asked a broader question:

> **Who traditionally supplies the capital needed to finance governments and markets?**

Several major pools of global savings were discussed.

Historically, important sources have included:

* Japan;
* oil-exporting economies;
* sovereign wealth funds;
* institutional investors;
* private savings.

But some of these flows are changing.

Japan is becoming relatively more attractive domestically as interest rates rise.

Oil-producing countries may also face different financial constraints when:

* oil revenues fall;
* infrastructure requires reconstruction;
* domestic investment needs increase.

The broader point is:

> **The supply of global liquidity is not constant.**

---

# 6. The Second Giant Consumer of Capital: AI

The class introduced a second major source of financing demand:

> **Artificial Intelligence infrastructure.**

Large technology companies are spending extraordinary amounts on:

* data centres;
* chips;
* computing infrastructure;
* energy;
* networks;
* supporting facilities.

This was introduced through the distinction between:

### CapEx — Capital Expenditure

Investment intended to create productive capacity.

### OpEx — Operating Expenditure

Recurring expenditure required to keep operations running.

Traditionally, CapEx resembles buying an asset that can be used over many years.

The analogy used in class was:

> Buying a house is CapEx; paying recurring rent is closer to OpEx.

But AI infrastructure creates an unusual situation.

Technology companies may need to replace or expand infrastructure continuously.

So what is formally classified as CapEx can begin to look economically more recurring than traditional capital investment.

---

# 7. Two Giants Competing for the Same Money

This led to one of the key macro-financial ideas of the session.

There are now two enormous consumers of capital:

1. **governments**, financing deficits and public debt;
2. **AI-related investment**, financing massive infrastructure requirements.

Both compete for the same global pools of savings.

Conceptually:

$$Global\ Savings \rightarrow \begin{cases} Government\ Debt\\ AI\ CapEx\\ Other\ Investments \end{cases}$$

If demand for capital increases faster than available savings, financing conditions can become tighter.

That can affect:

* interest rates;
* bond yields;
* valuations;
* corporate financing;
* government borrowing costs.

---

# 8. From Macro to Fixed Income

The class then moved from global liquidity to the basic mechanics of bonds.

A bond represents a contractual promise to make future payments.

At its simplest:

> **You give money today in exchange for receiving money in the future.**

This makes bond valuation a direct application of the time-value-of-money framework from Session 2.

---

# 9. Face Value

Suppose a bond promises to pay:

$\$100$$

at maturity.

That amount is called the **face value** or **par value**.

It is not necessarily the price of the bond today.

The key distinction is:

* **face value** = contractual amount paid at maturity;
* **market price** = what investors are willing to pay today.

These two numbers can be different.

---

# 10. Primary Market

The class illustrated the primary market through a simple auction.

Suppose the issuer needs financing and promises to pay:

$\$100$$

in two years.

An investor agrees to pay:

$\$91$$

today.

This is the **primary market**, because the bond is being issued for the first time.

Conceptually:

$$Issuer \rightarrow Bond \rightarrow Investor$$

and:

$$Investor \rightarrow Cash \rightarrow Issuer$$

The investor pays 91 today and receives 100 at maturity.

---

# 11. Yield to Maturity in the Simple Zero-Coupon Case

Using the Time Value of Money relationship:

$$FV=PV(1+r)^t$$

we can solve for the return:

$$r= \left( \frac{FV}{PV} \right)^{1/t}-1$$

For:

$$PV=91$$

$$FV=100$$

$$t=2$$

the annualized return is approximately:

$$r\approx4.83\%$$

This is the return the investor would receive if the bond were held until maturity under the assumptions of the example.

---

# 12. Is 4.83% a Good Return?

The answer is:

> **It depends.**

A return cannot be evaluated in isolation.

We need to compare it with:

* prevailing market rates;
* maturity;
* inflation expectations;
* risk-free rates;
* credit risk.

If a government security of similar maturity offers approximately the same return with very low credit risk, then lending to a riskier issuer should normally require a higher yield.

This introduces an important principle:

$$Higher\ Credit\ Risk \rightarrow Higher\ Required\ Yield$$

---

# 13. Credit Risk

Whenever buying a bond, an investor must ask:

> **Who is going to repay me?**

The promised return matters only if the issuer can actually make the payment.

Therefore, bond analysis always involves both:

* time value of money;
* credit risk.

This becomes especially important when yields move toward levels substantially above comparable low-risk government bonds.

Higher yields may represent:

* opportunity;
* additional risk;
* or both.

---

# 14. Secondary Market

Suppose the original investor buys the bond for 91.

One year later, before maturity, the investor needs cash.

The investor can sell the bond to someone else.

This transaction takes place in the **secondary market**.

The issuer is no longer directly involved.

The bond simply changes owners.

Thus:

### Primary Market

The issuer receives the financing.

### Secondary Market

Investors trade existing securities among themselves.

This distinction is fundamental to understanding financial markets.

---

# 15. Holding Period Return

Suppose the original investor:

* buys the bond for 91;
* sells it one year later for 94.

The return actually earned during that year is:

$$HPR= \frac{P_1-P_0}{P_0}$$

Therefore:

$$HPR= \frac{94-91}{91} \approx3.30\%$$

This is the **Holding Period Return (HPR)**.

It differs from the return originally expected if the investor had held the bond until maturity.

---

# 16. Different Investors Can Earn Different Returns from the Same Bond

The second investor buys the bond at 94.

If the bond pays 100 one year later:

$$Return= \frac{100-94}{94} \approx6.38\%$$

Therefore:

* Investor 1 earned approximately **3.30%**;
* Investor 2 earned approximately **6.38%**.

Yet both owned exactly the same bond at different moments.

This is an important lesson:

> **The realized return depends on the price at which you buy and sell the asset.**

---

# 17. Geometric Average Return

The class then connected the two annual returns with the original two-year return.

If:

$$r_1=3.30\%$$

and:

$$r_2=6.38\%$$

the geometric average is:

$\$1+\bar r = \sqrt{(1+r_1)(1+r_2)}$$

Therefore:

$$\boxed{\bar{r} = \sqrt{(1+r_1)(1+r_2)} - 1}$$

This brings us back to approximately the original annualized return of the investment.

The key intuition is:

> **Returns compound multiplicatively, not additively.**

---

# 18. Zero-Coupon Bonds

A **zero-coupon bond** makes no intermediate coupon payments.

The investor:

* pays a price today;
* receives the face value at maturity.

The price is therefore simply:

$$\boxed{P = \frac{FV}{(1+r)^t}}$$

For example, if a zero-coupon bond pays:

$\$1,000$$

in five years and the market rate is 5%:

$$P= \frac{1,000}{(1.05)^5}$$

The bond is purchased below face value and gradually approaches 1,000 as maturity approaches, assuming market rates remain unchanged.

---

# 19. Price Pulls to Par

One of the key fixed-income ideas introduced was:

> **Price pulls to par.**

If interest rates do not change, as a bond approaches maturity its market price tends toward its face value.

For a zero-coupon bond:

$$P_t \rightarrow FV \quad as\quad t\rightarrow maturity$$

The uncertainty caused by discounting gradually disappears because there is less time remaining until the final payment.

---

# 20. The Central Mantra Returns

The most repeated rule of the course appeared again:

$$\boxed{\text{Interest Rates}\uparrow \;\Rightarrow\; P_{\text{bond}}\downarrow}$$

and:

$$\boxed{\text{Interest Rates}\downarrow \;\Rightarrow\; P_{\text{bond}}\uparrow}$$

The explanation now follows directly from present-value mathematics.

If:

$$P= \frac{FV}{(1+r)^t}$$

then increasing \(r\) increases the denominator.

Therefore:

$$P\downarrow$$

This is no longer simply a rule to memorize.

Students can now understand why it happens.

---

# 21. Coupon Bonds

Most traditional bonds do not consist of only one final payment.

A **coupon bond** pays:

* periodic coupon payments;
* face value at maturity.

Suppose a three-year bond has:

$$Face\ Value=1,000$$

and an annual coupon rate of:

$\$6\%$$

The annual coupon is therefore:

$$C=60$$

Its cash flows are:

| Year | Cash Flow |
| ---- | --------: |
| 1    |        60 |
| 2    |        60 |
| 3    |     1,060 |

The bond price is simply the present value of these future cash flows.

---

# 22. Pricing a Coupon Bond

If the market discount rate is 5%:

$$P= \frac{60}{1.05} + \frac{60}{1.05^2} + \frac{1,060}{1.05^3}$$

Therefore:

$$\boxed{\text{Bond Price} = \text{PV of all future cash flows}}$$

This is the central valuation rule for fixed income.

Nothing fundamentally new has happened mathematically.

We are still applying the same principle introduced in Session 2.

---

# 23. Coupon Rate vs Market Yield

The class then examined what happens when the coupon rate differs from the market rate.

Suppose:

$$Coupon\ Rate=6\%$$

### If market yield = 6%

$$Price=Face\ Value$$

The bond trades **at par**.

### If market yield < 6%

The existing coupon is attractive relative to the market.

Therefore:

$$Price>Face\ Value$$

The bond trades at a **premium**.

### If market yield > 6%

The coupon is unattractive relative to newly available investments.

Therefore:

$$Price<Face\ Value$$

The bond trades at a **discount**.

---

# 24. Premium, Par and Discount Bonds

This gives a fundamental classification:

### Premium Bond

$$Coupon\ Rate>Market\ Yield$$

$$Price>Par$$

### Par Bond

$$Coupon\ Rate=Market\ Yield$$

$$Price=Par$$

### Discount Bond

$$Coupon\ Rate<Market\ Yield$$

$$Price<Par$$

This relationship is essential for understanding fixed-income markets.

---

# 25. A Coupon Bond as Two Components

A coupon bond can also be decomposed into:

1. an **annuity** consisting of the coupon payments;
2. a **zero-coupon bond** representing repayment of face value.

Therefore:

$$Bond = Annuity + Zero\ Coupon$$

This connects Session 3 directly with the annuity formulas introduced in Session 2.

The present value can therefore also be written as:

$$P= C \left[ \frac{1-(1+r)^{-t}}{r} \right] + \frac{FV}{(1+r)^t}$$

---

# 26. Price and Yield Are Two Ways of Quoting the Same Market

A subtle but important point was introduced.

In fixed income, traders may refer to a bond's “price” using either:

* its monetary market price;
* its yield.

For example, a market participant may quote a Treasury in yield terms rather than saying exactly how many dollars or euros it costs.

This happens because the price and yield are mathematically linked.

Knowing one allows us to calculate the other.

---

# 27. Price–Yield Relationship

The class then explored how the price of a coupon bond changes as interest rates change.

The result is:

$$r\uparrow \Rightarrow P\downarrow$$

but the relationship is **not linear**.

Instead, it is curved.

The class introduced the term:

> **convexity**

The price–yield relationship has a convex shape.

Students do not yet need to develop the mathematics in depth, but they should understand that equal changes in yield do not always generate exactly symmetrical changes in price.

---

# 28. First Introduction to Duration

The concept of **duration** was briefly introduced.

The basic question is:

> If interest rates change, **how much** will the price of the bond change?

The course mantra answers the direction:

$$Rates\uparrow \Rightarrow Prices\downarrow$$

Duration begins to answer the magnitude.

Conceptually:

$$Duration \approx Sensitivity\ of\ Bond\ Price\ to\ Interest\ Rates$$

This will become particularly important for fixed-income portfolio management.

# 30. Current Events and Rates

The session repeatedly connected bond-pricing mechanics with expectations about central-bank policy.

Prediction markets were again used as an example of how investors express expectations about future interest-rate decisions.

The important financial link is now explicit:

$$Expected\ Central\ Bank\ Decision \rightarrow Market\ Interest\ Rates \rightarrow Bond\ Prices$$

Thus, monetary-policy expectations become immediately relevant to fixed-income valuation.

# 32. Key Formulas

## Zero-Coupon Bond

$$\boxed{ P=\frac{FV}{(1+r)^t} }$$

---

## Yield on a Zero-Coupon Bond

$$\boxed{r = \left( \frac{FV}{P} \right)^{1/t} - 1}$$

---

## Coupon Bond

$$\boxed{P = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{C_t}{(1+r)^t} + \frac{FV}{(1+r)^n}}$$

For constant coupons:

$$\boxed{P = C \left[ \frac{1 - (1+r)^{-n}}{r} \right] + \frac{FV}{(1+r)^n}}$$

---

## Holding Period Return

For a simple price-only case:

$$\boxed{\text{HPR} = \frac{P_1 - P_0}{P_0}}$$

If coupons or other cash flows are received:

$$\boxed{\text{HPR} = \frac{P_1 - P_0 + \text{Income}}{P_0}}$$

---

## Geometric Average Return

$$\boxed{\bar{r} = \left[ \prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+r_i) \right]^{1/n} - 1}$$

For two periods:

$$\boxed{\bar{r} = \sqrt{(1+r_1)(1+r_2)} - 1}$$

---

# 33. Five Ideas to Leave the Room With

### 1. A bond is simply a collection of future cash flows.

Its value today is the present value of those payments.

### 2. Primary and secondary markets are different.

The primary market finances the issuer; the secondary market allows investors to trade existing securities.

### 3. Rates up → bond prices down.

This is now mathematically derived from present-value logic.

### 4. Coupon rate and market yield determine whether a bond trades at premium, par or discount.

$$Coupon>Yield \Rightarrow Premium$$

$$Coupon=Yield \Rightarrow Par$$

$$Coupon<Yield \Rightarrow Discount$$

### 5. The return you actually earn depends on when you sell.

Yield to maturity and holding-period return are not necessarily the same.

# Final Takeaway

Session 3 connected the macro picture with the mechanics of fixed income.

Governments and companies compete for scarce capital. Interest rates determine the price of that capital. Bonds transform those financing needs into contractual future cash flows.

Once those cash flows exist, the valuation logic is simple:

$$\boxed{\text{Price} = \text{Present Value of Future Cash Flows}}$$

Everything else — zero-coupon bonds, coupon bonds, yields, premium and discount pricing, HPR and eventually duration — grows from that single principle.
