# Financial Markets – Session 11: Midterm Feedback, Uniswap & Blockchain Infrastructure

## 1. Session Overview

Session 11 was the first class after the midterm.

The session had two closely connected objectives:

1. **Review several conceptual mistakes from the exam.**
    
2. Use one of those questions — particularly the reference to **Uniswap** — to introduce decentralized finance, blockchain and smart contracts.
    

The discussion moved through the following sequence:

$$
\text{Regulated  Markets} \longrightarrow \text{OTC} \longrightarrow \text{Uniswap} \longrightarrow \text{Wallets} \longrightarrow \text{Blockchain} \longrightarrow \text{Ethereum} \longrightarrow \text{Smart  Contracts}
$$
The main pedagogical message was simple:

> **Understand the concept before trying to produce a sophisticated answer.**

---

# PART I — MIDTERM FEEDBACK

## 2. First Principle: Answer the Question Being Asked

The class began by reviewing some exam answers.

One example concerned the difference between:

- an **OTC market**;
    
- a **supervised or regulated market**.
    

The professor emphasized that the question itself was conceptually simple.

A good answer did not require complicated terminology.

It required understanding the distinction.

The broader lesson was:

> **Before writing, identify exactly what the question is asking.**

Adding unrelated information can make an otherwise correct answer worse.

---

## 3. Regulated Markets

A regulated market operates within a formal institutional framework.

There are:

- rules;
    
- licenses;
    
- supervisory authorities;
    
- reporting requirements;
    
- mechanisms designed to limit abuse.
    

In the United States, the **SEC** was used in class as the main example of a market supervisor.

The classroom analogy was:

> A regulated activity is an activity in which someone is watching whether the rules are respected.

Some activities require supervision because inappropriate behaviour can harm other participants.

Financial markets are one example.

---

## 4. Why Supervision Exists

Financial markets contain asymmetries of:

- information;
    
- size;
    
- influence;
    
- market power.
    

A powerful participant may be able to affect prices through statements or actions.

Regulators therefore monitor activities such as:

- market manipulation;
    
- misuse of information;
    
- improper trading behaviour;
    
- compliance with market rules.
    

The general idea is:

$$
\text{Market  Power  
+  
Information  Asymmetry} \longrightarrow \text{Need  for  Supervision}
$$
---

## 5. OTC Markets

OTC means:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Over The Counter}
}
$$
An OTC transaction does not take place through the same standardized centralized market infrastructure as an exchange-traded transaction.

OTC markets can involve direct or decentralized relationships between participants.

The essential contrast used in class was:

### Regulated / centralized market

Formal market structure with explicit supervision.

### OTC

Trading outside that centralized exchange environment.

The important lesson from the exam was to explain this distinction directly rather than introduce unrelated meanings of the acronym OTC.

# PART III — UNISWAP FROM THE USER'S PERSPECTIVE

## 7. What Is Uniswap?

The class introduced Uniswap first from the perspective of a user.

The simplest definition used was:

> **Uniswap is an online decentralized protocol that allows users to exchange cryptoassets.**

A user can connect a cryptocurrency wallet and exchange one token for another.

For example:

$$
\text{ETH} \longrightarrow \text{USDT}
$$
The process can occur without using a conventional bank or centralized financial intermediary.

---

## 8. The Wallet

To use decentralized financial applications, a user needs a **wallet**.

The class compared a wallet conceptually with a bank deposit account:

> It is the place from which the user controls cryptoassets.

Technically, the important idea introduced was the relationship between:

- a **public key / address**;
    
- a **private key**.
    

The public address can be shared with others so they can send assets.

The private key must remain under the user's control.

Conceptually:

$$
\text{Public  Address} \longrightarrow \text{Where  assets  can  be  sent}
$$
$$
\text{Private  Key} \longrightarrow \text{Control  over  those  assets}
$$
The fundamental principle is:

> **Control of the private key means control of the wallet.**

---

## 9. Connecting a Wallet

The demonstration used a wallet such as MetaMask.

The simplified process is:

1. Open Uniswap.
    
2. Connect a wallet.
    
3. Select the token currently owned.
    
4. Select the token desired.
    
5. Specify the quantity.
    
6. Execute the transaction.
    

For example:

$$
\text{10  ETH} \longrightarrow \text{USDT}
$$
Once the transaction is completed, ETH leaves the user's holdings and the corresponding amount of USDT is received.

---

# PART IV — STABLECOINS

## 10. What Is USDT?

USDT — Tether — was introduced as a **stablecoin**.

A stablecoin attempts to maintain a relatively stable value against a reference asset.

In this case:

$$
1\ USDT \approx 1\ USD
$$
The purpose is to provide a blockchain-based token whose value is designed to remain close to the U.S. dollar.

---

## 11. Why Use a Stablecoin Instead of Dollars?

The classroom question was:

> If dollars already exist, why use USDT?

The key idea was that USDT operates within crypto and blockchain infrastructure.

A user can therefore remain inside that ecosystem while holding an asset intended to behave approximately like a dollar.

Conceptually:

$$
\text{Cryptoasset} \longrightarrow \text{Stablecoin}
$$
allows a user to change financial exposure without necessarily leaving the blockchain environment.

This distinction is important for understanding decentralized finance.

---

# PART V — UNISWAP AS FINANCIAL INFRASTRUCTURE

## 12. Uniswap Has Several Functions

One of the most important observations in the session was that Uniswap combines functions that are often separated in traditional finance.

It was presented from three perspectives:

1. a **swap mechanism**;
    
2. a **decentralized exchange**;
    
3. a form of **market-making infrastructure**.
    

This is one reason decentralized finance can be conceptually difficult at first.

Traditional financial categories begin to overlap.

---

## 13. Uniswap as a Swap Mechanism

At its simplest:

$$
\text{Asset  A} \longrightarrow \text{Asset  B}
$$
For example:

$$
\text{ETH} \longrightarrow \text{USDT}
$$
The user gives one asset and receives another.

The class used this intuitive meaning of "swap" before the formal derivatives chapter.

Later in the course, swaps will be studied more precisely as financial derivatives.

---

## 14. Uniswap as an Exchange

Uniswap also performs an exchange function.

Traditional example:

$$
NYSE
$$
Decentralized example:

$$
Uniswap
$$
But the institutional structures are very different.

A traditional exchange operates through:

- a company;
    
- formal rules;
    
- registered participants;
    
- regulators;
    
- centralized infrastructure.
    

A decentralized exchange operates through blockchain-based software and smart contracts.

Hence the abbreviation:

$$
\boxed{
DEX=Decentralized\ Exchange
}
$$
---

# PART VI — MARKET MAKERS

## 15. What Is a Market Maker?

The class then introduced another concept that will be important for the final exam:

> **Market maker**

A market maker contributes liquidity by being willing to buy and sell an asset.

Conceptually:

$$
Investor\ wants\ to\ buy  
\Rightarrow  
Market\ Maker\ can\ sell
$$
$$
Investor\ wants\ to\ sell  
\Rightarrow  
Market\ Maker\ can\ buy
$$
This reduces the difficulty of finding a counterparty for every transaction.

---

## 16. Why Market Makers Matter

Consider a highly liquid market such as U.S. Treasuries.

Liquidity does not appear magically.

Market infrastructure and participants help ensure that buyers and sellers can trade efficiently.

Market makers therefore contribute to:

- liquidity;
    
- continuous trading;
    
- observable prices;
    
- lower transaction frictions.
    

The general principle is:

$$
\text{Market  Making} \longrightarrow \text{Liquidity} \longrightarrow \text{Easier  Trading}
$$
---

## 17. Traditional vs Decentralized Market Making

In traditional regulated markets, market-making activities operate within a formal regulatory and institutional framework.

In decentralized finance, similar economic functions can be embedded in software.

This is one of the important conceptual differences introduced in the session:

> **Functions traditionally performed by several institutions can sometimes be combined inside a protocol.**

For the user, the complexity can disappear behind a simple interface:

$$
I\ have\ ETH
$$
$$
I\ want\ USDT
$$
$$
Swap
$$
The infrastructure handles the rest.

---

# PART VII — BLOCKCHAIN

## 18. Bitcoin and the Beginning of Blockchain

The historical discussion then moved back to Bitcoin and 2008.

Bitcoin introduced a practical use of blockchain technology for transferring a digital asset without requiring a conventional central intermediary.

The class simplified blockchain as:

> **a public and secure register**

The key concept is the register.

A financial system needs to know:

- who owns what;
    
- who transferred what;
    
- whether the same asset has already been spent;
    
- whether a transaction is valid.
    

Blockchain provides a mechanism for maintaining that information collectively.

---

## 19. Blockchain and Trust

This connected directly with the previous banking sessions.

Traditional finance frequently creates trust through institutions.

For example:

$$
\text{Depositor} \longrightarrow \text{Bank}
$$
or:

$$
\text{Commercial  Banks} \longrightarrow \text{Central  Bank}
$$
Blockchain proposes a different mechanism.

Instead of requiring the same centralized trusted intermediary, the system relies on:

- a shared ledger;
    
- cryptographic mechanisms;
    
- agreed protocol rules.
    

Conceptually:

### Traditional system

$$
\text{Trust} \longrightarrow \text{Institution}
$$
### Blockchain system

$$
\text{Trust} \longrightarrow \text{Protocol + Distributed  Record}
$$
The economic problem being addressed is similar:

> **How can people who do not know each other transact with confidence?**

---

# PART VIII — CENTRAL BANKS AND TRUST

## 20. The Central Bank Balance Sheet Revisited

The blockchain discussion led back to the central-bank balance sheet.

The class reviewed the distinction between assets and liabilities.

### Central-bank assets

Examples discussed during the course include:

- gold;
    
- government securities;
    
- foreign-currency assets.
    

### Central-bank liabilities

The key concept is:

$$
\boxed{
Monetary\ Base
}
$$
The monetary base is a liability of the central bank.

It includes central-bank money such as:

- currency in circulation;
    
- bank reserves.
    

Again:

$$
Monetary\ Base  
\neq  
Total\ Money\ Supply
$$
The broader money supply includes money created within the commercial banking system.

---

## 21. Gold and Confidence

Gold was again used as a historical example of an asset associated with confidence in monetary systems.

This connected back to the Bretton Woods discussion.

The broader theme was:

> Financial systems require mechanisms that allow people to trust records, money and counterparties.

Historically, these mechanisms have included:

- gold;
    
- governments;
    
- central banks;
    
- banks;
    
- financial regulation.
    

Blockchain introduces another possible architecture for establishing and recording ownership and transactions.

---

# PART IX — ETHEREUM

## 22. From Bitcoin to Ethereum

The class then introduced a major technological step beyond Bitcoin.

Bitcoin demonstrated blockchain-based digital money.

Ethereum extended the idea by allowing more flexible programs to operate on blockchain infrastructure.

The key concept was:

$$
\boxed{
Smart\ Contracts
}
$$
This creates a platform capable of doing more than simply recording transfers of one cryptocurrency.

---

## 23. What Is a Smart Contract?

The classroom definition was intentionally simple:

> **A smart contract is a program that executes automatically when specified conditions are satisfied.**

Despite the name:

- it does not need to be "smart";
    
- it is not necessarily a legal contract.
    

The important part is:

$$
If\ Conditions\ Are\ Met  
\Rightarrow  
Execute
$$
This is fundamentally a software concept.

---

## 24. Simple Smart Contract Logic

Imagine a program with the rule:

$$
IF\ payment\ received
$$
$$
THEN\ transfer\ token
$$
Once the defined conditions are satisfied, the program executes according to its code.

The blockchain provides the infrastructure on which that execution and transaction can be recorded.

---

## 25. Uniswap and Smart Contracts

This brings the discussion back to Uniswap.

A user interacts with a simple interface.

Behind that interface, smart contracts define how transactions occur.

Conceptually:

$$
\text{User} \longrightarrow \text{Wallet} \longrightarrow \text{Uniswap} \longrightarrow \text{Smart  Contract} \longrightarrow \text{Blockchain}
$$
The user does not need to understand every technical detail to perform the transaction.

But finance students should understand what infrastructure sits behind the interface.

---

# PART X — CENTRALIZED VS DECENTRALIZED FINANCE

## 26. Traditional Banking Architecture

Consider a conventional financial transaction.

Several institutions may be involved:

- banks;
    
- exchanges;
    
- brokers;
    
- clearing institutions;
    
- market makers;
    
- regulators.
    

Each performs a different function.

---

## 27. Decentralized Architecture

In decentralized finance, some of these economic functions can be performed through software protocols.

A simplified comparison is:

|Traditional Finance|Decentralized Finance|
|---|---|
|Bank account|Wallet|
|Centralized exchange|Decentralized exchange|
|Institutional infrastructure|Smart contracts|
|Central ledger|Blockchain ledger|
|Traditional intermediaries|Protocol-based execution|

The purpose of the comparison is not to claim that both systems are identical.

It is to understand which financial functions are being reproduced using different infrastructure.

# PART XIII — FIXED-INCOME FEEDBACK

## 32. Bond Pricing in the Midterm

The numerical exercise was also reviewed.

The fundamental requirement was to calculate the price of a bond.

The rule remains:

$$
\boxed{\text{Price} = \text{PV}(\text{Future Cash Flows})}
$$

Students needed to calculate the present value of:

- coupons;
    
- principal repayment.
    

Then add them:

$$
P=  
\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{C_t}{(1+r)^t}  
+  
\frac{FV}{(1+r)^n}
$$
The important lesson was that the individual present values are not the final answer.

Their sum is the bond price.

# Final Takeaway

Session 11 began with mistakes from the midterm and used them as a starting point for a new financial architecture.

The progression was:

$$
\text{OTC} \longrightarrow \text{Uniswap} \longrightarrow \text{Wallets} \longrightarrow \text{Blockchain} \longrightarrow \text{Ethereum} \longrightarrow \text{Smart  Contracts}
$$
At first, these concepts can appear completely different from traditional finance.

But underneath them we continue asking the same questions:

- Who owns the asset?
    
- Who records the transaction?
    
- Who provides liquidity?
    
- Who takes the other side of the trade?
    
- Who guarantees that the rules are followed?
    
- Where does trust come from?
    

That is why decentralized finance belongs in a Financial Markets course.

The technology is different.

The fundamental problems of finance remain the same.
