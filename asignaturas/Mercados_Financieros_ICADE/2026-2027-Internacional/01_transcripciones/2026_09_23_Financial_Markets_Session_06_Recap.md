# Financial Markets – Session 6: Investment Returns, Monetary Policy & Financial Crises

## 1. Session Overview

Session 6 connected three essential areas of finance: calculating investment returns, understanding how monetary policy works, and examining how financial crises emerge and spread.

The class began with a detailed review of Problem Set 1, particularly Exercise 7, before returning to the historical evolution of the monetary system.

The central question for the second half was:

Why did the relationship between interest rates, the monetary base and money supply change so dramatically after the 2008 financial crisis?

The session used the dot-com crash, the September 11 attacks, Lehman Brothers, the COVID-19 pandemic and Silicon Valley Bank to explain the relationship between liquidity, confidence, interest-rate risk and central-bank intervention.

## 2. Problem Set 1: Exercise 7

The first part of the session was dedicated to reviewing the exercises, particularly Exercise 7, which combined several measures of investment performance.

Consider a stock with the following year-end prices and dividends:

| Year | Stock price | Dividend |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2021 | \$100 | — |
| 2022 | \$72 | \$3 |
| 2023 | \$68 | \$3 |
| 2024 | \$88 | \$4 |
| 2025 | \$96 | \$5 |

The exercise asked students to calculate individual annual returns, arithmetic and geometric averages, the Internal Rate of Return (IRR), and the return obtained when dividends are reinvested.

### 2.1 Annual Holding Period Return

For a stock paying dividends:

$$\text{HPR}_t = \frac{P_t - P_{t-1} + D_t}{P_{t-1}}$$

The annual results are:

| Period | HPR |
| --- | --- |
| 2022 | −25.00% |
| 2023 | −1.39% |
| 2024 | 35.29% |
| 2025 | 14.77% |

The first-year return, for example, is:

$$\text{HPR}_{2022} = \frac{72 + 3 - 100}{100} = -25\%$$

Notice that receiving a dividend does not necessarily mean that an investor earns a positive return. The change in the stock price must also be considered.

### 2.2 Arithmetic Average Return

The arithmetic average is:

$$\bar{r}_A = \frac{r_1 + r_2 + r_3 + r_4}{4}$$

Arithmetic average

# 5.92%

This measures the simple average of the four annual returns. However, it does not tell us the actual compounded growth rate of the investment.

### 2.3 Geometric Average Return

Investment returns compound multiplicatively.

$$\bar{r}_G = \left[(1+r_1)(1+r_2)(1+r_3)(1+r_4)\right]^{1/4} - 1$$

Geometric average

# 3.52%

When using Excel, the geometric mean must be calculated from one plus each annual return, followed by subtracting one.

This produces the annualized compounded return.

### 2.4 Internal Rate of Return

The exercise then considered an investor who:

- buys one share for \$100;
    
- receives the four annual dividends as cash;
    
- sells the share for \$96 at the end of 2025.
    

The cash flows are:

| Year | Cash flow |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | −\$100 |
| 1 | \$3 |
| 2 | \$3 |
| 3 | \$4 |
| 4 | \$101 |

The IRR is the discount rate that makes the net present value of these cash flows equal to zero.

$$0 = -100 + \frac{3}{1+r} + \frac{3}{(1+r)^2} + \frac{4}{(1+r)^3} + \frac{101}{(1+r)^4}$$

Internal Rate of Return

# 2.77%

The result differs from the geometric average because the dividends are received as separate cash flows rather than automatically reinvested.

### 2.5 Reinvesting Dividends

Finally, suppose every dividend is reinvested in additional shares at the year-end ex-dividend price.

The number of shares owned increases progressively:

$$N_t = N_{t-1}\left(1 + \frac{D_t}{P_t}\right)$$

The investor starts with one share and uses each dividend to purchase additional fractional shares.

At the end of the investment period, total wealth reflects the value of all shares accumulated through reinvestment.

In this particular example, the annualized return matches the geometric average of the annual total returns: approximately 3.52%.

## Three different measures, three different answers

Key lesson: Always understand what happens to intermediate cash flows before choosing a measure of investment performance.

## 5. Three Graphs, One Monetary History

The professor returned to the three graphs introduced at the end of Session 5.

## The three variables

Interest rates

The price of short-term money and a central instrument of monetary policy.

Monetary base

Money issued by the central bank, including currency in circulation and commercial-bank reserves.

Money supply (M2)

A broader measure of money available in the economy, including currency and specified categories of bank deposits.

The relationship between these variables changes significantly when a financial system enters a crisis.

The class distinguished two periods: the monetary system before 2008 and the system that developed after the global financial crisis.

## 6. Monetary Policy Before 2008

Before 2008, the conventional monetary-policy cycle could be represented through the following sequence.

Economic expansion and rising inflation

Central bank raises interest rates

Credit becomes more expensive and economic activity slows

Recession or economic slowdown

Central bank cuts interest rates to support recovery

This was a simplified representation of conventional countercyclical monetary policy, not a mechanical rule that every economy always follows.

The class used the historical interest-rate chart to identify periods of tightening, recession and recovery.

The important distinction was that before 2008, most visible monetary-policy adjustments occurred through changes in interest rates rather than enormous expansions of central-bank balance sheets.

## 7. The Internet, the Dot-Com Bubble and September 11

The historical explanation then returned to the development of the internet.

The professor emphasized the internet's importance in connecting financial markets and accelerating the transmission of information.

The dot-com boom of the late 1990s attracted substantial investment into technology companies. When expectations reversed, many valuations collapsed and monetary policy became more accommodative.

Following the terrorist attacks of 11 September 2001, uncertainty increased further.

The class connected these events with the subsequent period of low U.S. interest rates and the expansion of mortgage lending.

The simplified sequence was:

Dot-com collapse and the 2001 shock

Lower interest rates

Cheaper credit and expanding mortgage lending

Housing-market expansion and increasing leverage

Rising financial vulnerabilities

The lesson was not that low interest rates alone caused the financial crisis. Lending standards, securitization, excessive leverage and weaknesses in financial supervision were also important.

Instead, the class highlighted how prolonged financing conditions can influence risk-taking throughout the financial system.

## 8. Lehman Brothers and the Collapse of Confidence

The turning point was the bankruptcy of Lehman Brothers on 15 September 2008.

![](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.federalreservehistory.org&sz=32)

Federal Reserve History

The class explained how banks depended on confidence and short-term funding to maintain their daily operations.

Banks routinely lend money to one another through the interbank market.

But once confidence in counterparties disappears, institutions become reluctant to lend, even when the immediate borrowing need is short term.

The result can be a liquidity crisis affecting the entire financial system.

The professor used an analogy involving two connected reservoirs of water.

Under normal circumstances, liquidity flows between financial institutions. When confidence collapses, those flows can suddenly stop.

Without emergency intervention, otherwise functioning businesses and institutions may lose access to the funding they need.

The important principle is:

A problem initially affecting one institution can become systemic when confidence and interbank liquidity disappear.

## 9. The Federal Reserve's Response

The session presented the Federal Reserve's intervention in 2008 as a major turning point in modern monetary policy.

The Fed responded with emergency liquidity facilities, very substantial interest-rate reductions and subsequent asset purchases.

This illustrated that expanding central-bank money does not automatically produce an equivalent expansion in commercial-bank lending, deposits or consumer spending.

The ratio used in class is a simplified illustration, rather than a fixed mechanical money multiplier. In a crisis, banks and other economic agents may prefer to hold liquidity rather than expand lending.

## 10. Why Didn't the Initial Intervention Produce Immediate Inflation?

This was one of the most important questions raised in the session.

When the Federal Reserve expanded its balance sheet in response to the crisis, much of the newly created central-bank money remained within the financial system as bank reserves.

The class distinguished between creating central-bank liquidity and increasing spending by households and businesses.

If additional liquidity is primarily held by banks rather than circulating through new loans and spending, the immediate inflationary consequences may be limited.

This is essential for understanding why the monetary base and broader money supply can behave very differently.

The distinction also helps explain why monetary-policy transmission depends on the condition and behaviour of the banking system.

## 11. Monetary Policy After 2008

The session then explored how unconventional monetary policy became more prominent after the crisis.

Central banks increasingly used their balance sheets to support financial conditions, especially when interest rates approached very low levels.

The class referred to successive episodes of extraordinary monetary intervention, including the European sovereign-debt crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic.

## A changing monetary-policy framework

The main lesson is that central-bank balance sheets became an increasingly important part of monetary-policy analysis.

## 12. COVID-19, Stimulus and Inflation

The COVID-19 period provided a useful contrast with the 2008 crisis.

During the pandemic, the United States used direct fiscal transfers to households, while the Federal Reserve provided substantial monetary accommodation.

Unlike reserves held inside the banking system, fiscal transfers can increase households' spendable income directly.

The class connected the expansion of money and purchasing power with the subsequent rise in inflation.

However, it is important to distinguish between the two policies: stimulus checks were government fiscal transfers, not direct payments issued by the Federal Reserve.

The inflationary effects of pandemic-era policies also interacted with supply disruptions, changes in consumption patterns and energy prices.

## 13. Inflation, Rising Rates and Bond Losses

When inflation became elevated, central banks responded by increasing interest rates.

This returned the class to its most frequently repeated financial relationship:

$$\boxed{\text{Interest Rates}\uparrow \;\Rightarrow\; \text{Bond Prices}\downarrow}$$

This relationship is fundamental for understanding why rapidly rising interest rates can create problems for financial institutions holding large fixed-income portfolios.

A bond portfolio can be exposed to considerable interest-rate risk even when its securities have relatively low credit risk.

This distinction led directly to the final case study of the session.

## 14. Silicon Valley Bank: Interest-Rate Risk Meets Liquidity Risk

Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) was used to demonstrate how rising interest rates can interact with a bank's funding structure.

The bank held substantial amounts of securities whose market values fell as interest rates increased.

At the same time, customers began withdrawing deposits. To meet withdrawals, SVB sold securities at a loss, and a rapid bank run followed. The bank failed on 10 March 2023.

![](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.fdic.gov&sz=32)

FDIC.gov

+1

Two types of risk were particularly relevant.

Interest-rate risk: higher yields reduce the market value of existing fixed-rate bonds.

Liquidity risk: depositors may demand money more quickly than a bank can obtain cash without incurring significant losses.

These risks can reinforce each other. If a bank must sell long-term assets at depressed prices to satisfy immediate withdrawals, unrealized market losses can become realized losses.

The SVB case also illustrated the importance of depositor concentration and confidence.

## 15. Why the Speed of Intervention Matters

The class concluded by comparing policy responses across financial crises.

The professor used the Great Depression, Lehman Brothers and Silicon Valley Bank to illustrate how financial authorities have developed increasingly extensive tools for addressing liquidity stress.

The central question was:

> How quickly should monetary authorities respond when a financial institution's difficulties threaten to spread through the wider system?

The discussion highlighted the role of central banks as emergency liquidity providers and the importance of maintaining confidence.

It also sets up an important distinction for future sessions: a liquidity problem is not necessarily the same as insolvency. Providing additional funding cannot, by itself, repair an institution whose assets are insufficient to cover its liabilities.

## 16. Current Markets: Capital Competition and AI

Alongside the technical material, the class returned to the themes developed in Sessions 3–5.

Governments, technology companies and other borrowers compete for financing, while the international flow of savings can change as interest rates and investment opportunities evolve.

An AI-generated video was used as an illustration of competition among major technology companies and the enormous capital requirements associated with AI infrastructure.

The professor connected this example with the broader objective of the course: students should not rely exclusively on a static syllabus when significant financial developments are unfolding around them.

The aim is to combine historical understanding, numerical skills, current information and critical thinking.

## 18. Five Ideas to Leave the Room With

1

Returns compound multiplicatively.

The arithmetic average, geometric average and IRR answer different questions.

2

Monetary base and money supply are different.

Creating bank reserves does not necessarily produce an equivalent increase in spending or credit.

3

Financial systems depend on confidence.

A loss of trust can rapidly disrupt interbank funding and create systemic liquidity problems.

4

Interest-rate risk and liquidity risk can reinforce each other.

Silicon Valley Bank illustrated how falling bond values and deposit withdrawals can combine.

5

Financial crises must be understood in their historical context.

The 2008 crisis transformed central-bank intervention and continues to influence monetary policy.
