# Financial Markets – Session 12: Derivatives, Options, VIX & Insurance

## 1. Session Overview

Session 12 connected three major ideas:

1. **Uncertainty and financial decision-making**.
2. **Derivatives, options and implied volatility**.
3. **Insurance companies and risk management**.

The session began with the recurring definition of finance:

> **Finance is about managing an unpredictable future.**

From there, the class moved to a practical question:

> If the future is uncertain, can markets tell us how much uncertainty investors are pricing?

This led to:

$$
Derivatives \rightarrow Options \rightarrow Volatility \rightarrow VIX
$$

The second half of the session connected options with insurance contracts and then compared the structure of the **banking system** with the **insurance sector**.

The central idea was:

> **Options and insurance are different instruments, but both are mechanisms for transferring risk.**

---

# PART I — FINANCE, UNCERTAINTY & DECISIONS

## 2. Finance Is About the Future

The session returned to one of the most repeated ideas of the course:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Finance = Managing  an  Unpredictable  Future}
}
$$

Financial decisions are made today, but their consequences occur in the future.

Therefore, finance requires:

- information;
- expectations;
- risk assessment;
- understanding how the system works.

The objective is not to predict the future perfectly.

The objective is to make better decisions under uncertainty.

---

## 3. Risk and Uncertainty

The class distinguished conceptually between the future we do not know and the risks associated with that uncertainty.

The important idea is:

$$
\text{Future
 rightarrow
Uncertainty
 rightarrow
Risk
 rightarrow
Financial  Decisions}
$$

Financial instruments allow economic agents to:

- take risk;
- transfer risk;
- hedge risk;
- price risk.

This provides the bridge to derivatives and insurance.

# PART II — GO BACK TO THE BASICS

## 4. A Financial Instrument

Before discussing options, the class returned to the basic definition of a financial instrument.

A financial instrument can be understood as a contractual relationship between parties.

From the accounting perspective introduced in class:

> A financial instrument is generally an asset for one party and a liability or equity instrument for another.

The important point is that finance is built around contracts.

---

## 5. Three Characteristics of Financial Instruments

The class repeated three fundamental dimensions for analysing financial instruments:

$$
\boxed{
Return
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
Risk
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
Liquidity
}
$$

Almost every financial asset can be analysed through these three questions:

1. What return can I expect?
2. What risk am I accepting?
3. How easily can I convert the position into cash?

---

## 6. Main Types of Financial Instruments

The class organized financial instruments into broad categories.

### Cash

Very high liquidity and very low nominal price volatility.

### Financing instruments

Examples:

- bonds;
- stocks.

### Derivatives

Financial instruments whose value depends on another asset, variable or financial instrument.

Conceptually:

$$
\text{Derivative  Value
=
f(Underlying)}
$$

# PART III — DERIVATIVES

## 7. What Is a Derivative?

A derivative is a financial instrument whose value depends on an underlying asset or variable.

Examples of underlying assets may include:

- stocks;
- bonds;
- interest rates;
- currencies;
- commodities;
- market indices.

The key idea is:

$$
Underlying\ Changes
\Rightarrow
Derivative\ Value\ Changes
$$

---

## 8. Main Types of Derivatives

The session introduced the main families:

- options;
- swaps;
- forwards;
- futures.

The objective was not to master all of them immediately.

The purpose was to create a conceptual map.

A simplified structure is:

$$
Derivatives
$$

$$
\downarrow
$$

$$
\text{Options  quad Swaps  quad Forwards  quad Futures}
$$

This vocabulary will become increasingly important later in the course.

# PART IV — OPTIONS

## 9. What Is an Option?

An option gives its buyer a **right**, but not an obligation.

This distinction is essential.

The holder pays for the possibility of taking an action later.

The holder is not forced to exercise the option.

---

## 10. Call Option

A **call option** gives the holder:

> **the right to buy**

an underlying asset at a predetermined price.

Conceptually:

$$
Call = Right\ to\ Buy
$$

If the market price rises sufficiently above the agreed price, the right becomes valuable.

---

## 11. Put Option

A **put option** gives the holder:

> **the right to sell**

an underlying asset at a predetermined price.

Conceptually:

$$
Put = Right\ to\ Sell
$$

A put can therefore provide protection against a fall in the price of an asset.

---

## 12. Strike Price

The contractual price at which the holder can buy or sell the underlying asset is called the:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Strike  Price}
}
$$

For example, suppose a call gives the right to buy an asset for:

$$
K=100
$$

If the market price later becomes:

$$
S=105
$$

the holder has the right to buy for 100 something that is worth 105 in the market.

Ignoring the premium and other details, that right has value.

---

## 13. Rights and Obligations

This asymmetry is fundamental.

### Option buyer

Has a **right**.

### Option seller

Has an **obligation** if the buyer exercises the option.

This structure will later help connect options with insurance contracts.

---

# PART V — OPTION PRICING & VOLATILITY

## 14. How Do We Price an Option?

The class briefly introduced the idea that option pricing is more complex than ordinary present-value calculations.

The **Black-Scholes framework** was mentioned as a major option-pricing model.

The formula itself was not developed in detail during this session.

The important conceptual variable was:

$$
\boxed{
Volatility
}
$$

---

## 15. Historical Volatility

Historical volatility uses past price movements.

The basic idea is:

$$
\text{Past  Price  Movements
 rightarrow
Estimate  of  Volatility}
$$

The past is observable.

The future is not.

Historical data can therefore be used as one input when thinking about future uncertainty.

---

## 16. Implied Volatility

The class then reversed the problem.

Suppose we observe:

- today's stock price;
- today's option price.

If the market price of the option is already known, we can infer the volatility consistent with that price.

This is called:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Implied  Volatility}
}
$$

Conceptually:

$$
\text{Option  Price
+
Underlying  Price
 rightarrow
Implied  Volatility}
$$

Implied volatility therefore reflects the volatility embedded in market prices.

---

# PART VI — THE VIX

## 17. What Is the VIX?

The class introduced the VIX as a market measure derived from option prices.

The intuition presented was:

> **The VIX reflects the volatility that the options market is pricing.**

It therefore provides information about expected market uncertainty.

---

## 18. Why the VIX Matters

The class began by asking whether uncertainty could somehow be observed in financial markets.

The VIX provides one possible answer.

If option prices imply higher volatility:

$$
Implied\ Volatility\uparrow
$$

then the VIX will generally be higher.

Conceptually:

$$
Market\ Fear/Uncertainty\uparrow
\Rightarrow
Option\ Protection\ More\ Valuable
\Rightarrow
Implied\ Volatility\uparrow
$$

The VIX is therefore often interpreted as a market measure of expected volatility.

---

## 19. Expectations Matter More Than the Event Itself

The class used current events to illustrate an important financial-market principle.

A major event does not necessarily generate a large market movement if investors had already anticipated it.

This is the same logic discussed previously with:

- interest rates;
- central-bank communication;
- forward guidance.

Financial prices incorporate expectations.

Therefore:

$$
Expected\ Event
\neq
Necessarily\ Large\ Market\ Surprise
$$

What matters is often the difference between:

$$
\text{What  Happened}
$$

and:

$$
\text{What  Markets  Expected}
$$

# PART IX — OPTIONS AND INSURANCE

## 26. The Main Connection

The class asked:

> What does an option contract have in common with an insurance contract?

The first major similarity is:

$$
\boxed{
Premium
}
$$

In both cases, the buyer pays something today in exchange for protection or a right that may become valuable later.

---

## 27. Insurance Structure

A simplified insurance contract works as follows:

$$
\text{Premium  Paid  Today}
$$

$$
\downarrow
$$

$$
Wait
$$

$$
\downarrow
$$

$$
\text{Covered  Event  Occurs}
$$

$$
\downarrow
$$

$$
Payment/Claim
$$

The insured pays upfront for protection against an uncertain future event.

---

## 28. Option Structure

An option follows a similar structure:

$$
\text{Premium  Paid  Today}
$$

$$
\downarrow
$$

$$
Wait
$$

$$
\downarrow
$$

$$
\text{Relevant  Market  Event}
$$

$$
\downarrow
$$

$$
\text{Exercise  Right}
$$

The mechanics are therefore structurally similar.

---

## 29. Insurance and Put Options

The class identified a particularly useful comparison:

> **A put option resembles insurance against a fall in an asset's value.**

Suppose an investor owns a stock.

The investor buys a put giving the right to sell at a predetermined price.

If the stock price falls dramatically, the put becomes valuable.

This resembles insurance protection.

$$
Asset\ Price\ Falls
\Rightarrow
Put\ Protection\ Becomes\ Valuable
$$

This is often a more intuitive comparison than using a call option.

---

## 30. Buyer Rights and Seller Obligations

Another similarity is the legal/economic structure.

### Buyer

Pays the premium and obtains a right.

### Seller / insurer

Receives the premium and accepts a contingent obligation.

Conceptually:

$$
Buyer
=
Right
$$

$$
\text{Seller
=
Conditional  Obligation}
$$

This is one of the strongest structural similarities between options and insurance.

---

## 31. More Similarities

Other parallels discussed included:

- upfront premium;
- expiration period;
- contingent payment;
- protection against adverse events;
- strike price versus contractual thresholds or deductibles.

The purpose was not to claim that options and insurance are identical.

The purpose was to recognize that both are mechanisms for:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Risk  Transfer}
}
$$

# PART X — FROM BANKING TO INSURANCE

## 32. Why Do We Say "Banking System"?

The class then returned to banking.

We normally speak of:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Banking  System}
}
$$

But more often of:

$$
\boxed{
Insurance\ Sector
}
$$

Why?

The class used the concept of a **system**.

---

## 33. What Makes a System a System?

A system consists of interconnected components that behave as part of a whole.

The human body was used as an analogy.

An isolated arm is not a functioning body.

Within the body, however, it interacts with:

- circulation;
- nerves;
- muscles;
- other organs.

The parts are connected.

Therefore:

$$
\text{System
=
Interconnected  Components}
$$

---

## 34. The Banking System as a Circulatory System

The class compared the banking system with the circulatory system.

Banks are interconnected.

At the centre sits the central bank.

The analogy was:

$$
Central\ Bank
\approx
Heart
$$

The central bank influences the flow of liquidity throughout the financial system.

Banks also interact through:

- payments;
- funding markets;
- reserves;
- lending;
- interbank relationships.

This interconnectedness explains why the failure of one institution can potentially affect others.

---

## 35. Systemic Risk

Because banks are interconnected:

$$
\text{Problem  in  One  Bank}
$$

can potentially become:

$$
\text{Problem  Across  the  System}
$$

This is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Systemic  Risk}
}
$$

This idea was already visible in the discussions of:

- Lehman Brothers;
- bank runs;
- Silicon Valley Bank;
- central-bank intervention.

# PART XI — THE INSURANCE CONTRACT

## 36. Main Participants

The insurance contract was divided into several roles.

### Policyholder

The person who contracts the insurance and pays the premium.

### Insured

The person or asset exposed to the insured risk.

### Beneficiary

The person who receives the payment when the contractual conditions are satisfied.

### Insurance company

The institution accepting the risk.

---

## 37. One Person Can Play Several Roles

The policyholder, insured and beneficiary can sometimes be the same person.

Example:

### Health insurance

A person may:

- contract the policy;
- be the insured;
- receive the benefit.

Therefore:

$$
Policyholder
=
Insured
=
Beneficiary
$$

---

## 38. Different People Can Play Different Roles

In life insurance, the roles may differ.

For example:

- one person contracts the policy;
- that person is insured;
- other people are beneficiaries.

Therefore:

$$
Policyholder
=
Insured
$$

but:

$$
Beneficiary
\neq
Insured
$$

Understanding these roles is important when analysing insurance contracts.

---

# PART XII — INSURANCE COMPANIES AS FINANCIAL INSTITUTIONS

## 39. Premiums Come Before Claims

From the financial perspective, insurance companies receive premiums before many future claims are paid.

Conceptually:

$$
Premiums \rightarrow Insurance\ Company
$$

then later:

$$
Insurance\ Company \rightarrow Claims
$$

This creates a large pool of financial assets.

---

## 40. Insurance Companies Are Major Investors

Because premiums are collected before claims are paid, insurance companies manage substantial investment portfolios.

They therefore become major institutional investors.

They can hold:

- bonds;
- equities;
- real estate;
- other financial assets.

This is why insurance companies are important not only as risk-transfer institutions but also as participants in financial markets.

---

# PART XIII — COINSURANCE & REINSURANCE

## 41. Coinsurance

The class compared **coinsurance** with syndicated lending.

In a syndicated loan, several banks jointly finance a large borrower.

Similarly, in coinsurance, several insurers can participate in the same risk.

Conceptually:

$$
\text{Large  Risk}
$$

$$
\downarrow
$$

$$
\text{Insurer  A
+
Insurer  B
+
Insurer  C}
$$

The risk is distributed among several institutions.

---

## 42. Reinsurance

Reinsurance is different.

An insurance company can transfer part of the risk it has accepted to another insurer.

Conceptually:

$$
Customer \rightarrow Insurance\ Company \rightarrow Reinsurer
$$

The original customer has a contract with the insurer.

The insurer then protects itself by purchasing insurance from another insurance company.

Therefore:

> **Reinsurance is insurance for insurers.**

---

## 43. Why Reinsurance Exists

Reinsurance allows insurance companies to:

- reduce concentration risk;
- increase underwriting capacity;
- protect themselves against exceptionally large losses;
- diversify risk.

It is another example of risk being transferred through financial contracts.

---

# PART XIV — BANKS VS INSURANCE COMPANIES

## 44. Similarities

Banks and insurance companies have several important characteristics in common.

Both are:

- financial intermediaries;
- large investors;
- balance-sheet institutions;
- highly regulated;
- professional risk managers.

Both must understand:

$$
Assets
$$

$$
Liabilities
$$

and:

$$
Risk
$$

---

## 45. A Major Difference: Interconnection

The class emphasized that the banking business is more naturally described as a **system**.

Banks are directly interconnected through monetary and payment infrastructure.

Insurance companies also interact and can face common shocks, but the institutional structure differs.

This helps explain why the language of:

$$
\text{Banking  System}
$$

is particularly important.

---

## 46. Maturity Transformation

Banks perform substantial maturity transformation.

They may finance:

$$
\text{Long text{-}Term  Loans}
$$

using:

$$
\text{Short text{-}Term  Deposits}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\text{Short  Liabilities
 rightarrow
Long  Assets}
$$

This creates:

- liquidity risk;
- interest-rate risk;
- systemic vulnerability.

---

## 47. Time and Banking

Time Value of Money is especially important in banking.

A change in interest rates today may affect:

- bonds;
- mortgages;
- loans;
- deposits;
- future cash flows;

for many years.

Therefore:

$$
Interest\ Rate\ Change\ Today
\rightarrow
Multi\text{-}Year\ Impact
$$

This is why duration will remain important for the final exam.

# PART XV — INSURANCE MATHEMATICS

## 48. Insurance Focuses Strongly on Probability

The mathematical emphasis in insurance differs from the fixed-income focus developed earlier in the course.

Fixed income frequently emphasizes:

$$
\text{Present  Value}
$$

Insurance frequently requires substantial use of:

$$
Probability
$$

and:

$$
Statistics
$$

because future claims are uncertain.

---

## 49. Mortality and Survival

Life insurance provides a simple example.

If:

$$
q_x
$$

represents a probability of death over a given period, then the corresponding survival probability can be represented conceptually as:

$$
p_x=1-q_x
$$

Insurance mathematics therefore requires estimating probabilities associated with:

- survival;
- mortality;
- accidents;
- claims.

---

## 50. Law of Large Numbers

Insurance becomes more predictable when many similar independent risks are pooled.

A single claim is uncertain.

A sufficiently large portfolio can become statistically more predictable.

Conceptually:

$$
\text{Many  Risks
 rightarrow
More  Predictable  Aggregate  Losses}
$$

This is one of the fundamental ideas behind insurance.
