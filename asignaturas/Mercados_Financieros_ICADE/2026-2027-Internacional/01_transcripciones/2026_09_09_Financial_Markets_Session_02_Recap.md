# Financial Markets – Session 2: Time Value of Money & AI-Assisted Learning

## 1. Session Overview

The second session combined two complementary dimensions of the course:

1. **How to learn and work with information using AI tools.**
    
2. **The mathematical foundation of finance: the Time Value of Money.**
    

The main financial objective was to become comfortable with one fundamental relationship:

$$FV = PV(1+r)^t$$

and therefore:

$$PV = \frac{FV}{(1+r)^t}$$

Almost everything introduced during the second half of the session — returns, multiple cash flows, perpetuities, annuities and, eventually, bond valuation — can be understood as an extension of this basic idea.

The exercises can be discussed with classmates, but students were strongly encouraged to work through them individually.

The purpose is not simply assessment. The exercises should work as a **KPI — Key Performance Indicator** of each student's understanding.

In other words:

> The exercises tell you whether you actually understand the material.

---

# 3. Finance and KPIs

The idea of a KPI was used to connect everyday performance measurement with financial analysis.

Finance constantly uses indicators to identify whether something important is changing.

One example discussed was the behaviour of long-term government bond yields in the United States and Japan.

The class compared the two markets and highlighted the significant change taking place in Japan.

Japanese interest rates had previously been extremely low — and at times negative — while long-term yields have recently moved substantially higher.

The class noted that the Japanese 10-year government bond yield had recently approached **3%**.

The important point was not the exact number itself.

It was the **signal**.

Higher Japanese yields imply that:

- financing is becoming more expensive in Japan;
    
- Japanese assets become relatively more attractive to investors;
    
- capital-flow incentives may change;
    
- strategies based on extremely cheap yen financing may become less attractive.
    

This led back to the **carry trade**, introduced briefly in Session 1.

For many years, Japan provided exceptionally cheap financing to global markets.

If Japanese rates structurally increase, that mechanism may begin to change.

---

# 4. Signals and an Unpredictable Future

Other market developments — U.S. public debt, interest rates, IPO activity and technological investment — were also mentioned as signals.

But a signal is not the same thing as certainty.

This reinforced one of the central ideas from Session 1:

> **Finance is about managing an unpredictable future.**

We cannot know exactly what will happen.

What we can do is:

- collect information;
    
- identify signals;
    
- interpret them;
    
- assign probabilities;
    
- manage risk accordingly.

# 8. Source-Grounded AI and Hallucinations

One particularly useful feature discussed was the ability of NotebookLM to work around a defined set of sources.

When the system was asked about **Cristiano Ronaldo**, the class material did not contain relevant information, so the tool indicated that the available sources did not support an answer.

This illustrated an important issue in generative AI:

> **hallucination**

An LLM can sometimes produce plausible information even when it does not actually know the answer.

Source-grounded systems can reduce this risk.

However, the class also produced an excellent warning immediately afterwards: an AI-generated podcast incorrectly referred to the class as taking place in **2027**, despite the source being from 2026.

Therefore:

> **AI outputs must still be checked.**

> Never fully automate the delivery of important information without reviewing it.

# 11. The Time Value of Money

The financial core of the class began with a very simple example.

Suppose you invest:

$$PV = 1,000$$

at an annual interest rate of:

$$r = 5\%$$

After one year:

$$FV = 1,000(1.05) = 1,050$$

This gives the general future value formula:

$$\boxed{FV = PV(1+r)^t}$$

where:

- $PV$ = Present Value
    
- $FV$ = Future Value
    
- $r$ = interest rate
    
- $t$ = number of periods
    

Rearranging:

$$\boxed{PV = \frac{FV}{(1+r)^t}}$$

This is the fundamental formula of the session.

---

# 12. Compounding

The reason time matters is **compounding**.

Returns generated in one period can themselves generate returns in later periods.

This produces:

$$PV \rightarrow PV(1+r) \rightarrow PV(1+r)^2 \rightarrow PV(1+r)^3 \rightarrow \cdots$$

The class connected this idea with studying.

If all the work is left until the day before the exam, there is very little time for previous learning to reinforce later learning.

If students begin early, knowledge can accumulate progressively.

The analogy is:

> **Knowledge compounds just as capital compounds.**

---

# 13. Small Differences Become Large Differences Over Time

A historical example involving the purchase of **New York** was used to illustrate the enormous effect of compounding over several centuries.

The exact historical details were less important than the mathematical lesson.

Over very long periods:

> **a one-percentage-point difference in return can create an enormous difference in final value.**

Time dramatically magnifies apparently small differences in annual returns.

---

# 14. Solving the Formula in Different Directions

Students should not memorize many disconnected formulas.

Instead, they should understand one relationship and learn to solve for different variables.

Starting from:

$$FV = PV(1+r)^t$$

the problem may ask for:

- $FV$;
    
- $PV$;
    
- $r$;
    
- $t$.
    

The professor described this as the basic **“name of the game”**:

> Know two or three variables and calculate the missing one.

---

# 15. Calculating the Rate of Return

Starting with:

$$FV = PV(1+r)^t$$

we obtain:

$$(1+r)^t = \frac{FV}{PV}$$

Therefore:

$$\boxed{r = \left(\frac{FV}{PV}\right)^{1/t} - 1}$$

This allows us to calculate the return generated between a present value and a future value.

A simple practice question mentioned in class was:

> If you invest €1,000 and later receive €1,100, what return have you earned?

---

# 16. Calculating Time

Sometimes the unknown is the number of periods.

The class considered an investor aged 40 who has:

$$PV = \$500,000$$

and wants to reach:

$$FV = \$4,000,000$$

assuming an annual return of:

$$r = 8\%$$

The relationship is:

$\$4,000,000 = 500,000(1.08)^t$$

Therefore:

$\$8 = (1.08)^t$$

To isolate $t\$, logarithms can be used:

$$\boxed{t = \frac{\log(FV/PV)}{\log(1+r)}}$$

The objective is not to memorize the logarithmic expression.

The important point is to understand how the original present-value/future-value relationship is being rearranged.

---

# 17. Multiple Cash Flows

Real financial assets usually do not produce only one future cash flow.

Suppose an investment generates several future payments:

CF1, CF2, CF3,…,CFnCF_1,\ CF_2,\ CF_3,\ldots,CF_n

Each cash flow must be discounted separately:

$$PV_1 = \frac{CF_1}{1+r} \qquad PV_2 = \frac{CF_2}{(1+r)^2}$$

and so on.

The total value becomes:

$$\boxed{PV = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t}}$$

This is the basic architecture of **Discounted Cash Flow valuation**.

---

# 18. Office Building Example

The class considered an office building that could generate rental income of approximately:

40,00040,000

per year.

If the building is also sold in the final year, the last cash flow includes:

- the final rental payment;
    
- the sale price.
    

The value of the building today is calculated by discounting each future payment back to the present.

For example:

$$PV = \frac{40}{1.06} + \frac{40}{1.06^2} + \frac{40}{1.06^3} + \frac{40}{1.06^4} + \frac{540}{1.06^5}$$

using the simplified figures presented during class.

The principle is fundamental:

> **The price of an asset today is connected with the present value of the cash flows it will generate in the future.**

---

# 19. Why This Matters for Bonds

The professor explicitly connected Time Value of Money with the later study of **fixed income**.

A bond is essentially a collection of future cash flows.

Therefore, pricing a bond means:

1. identifying its future payments;
    
2. discounting those payments;
    
3. adding their present values.
    

This is why mastering PV and FV is essential before studying bonds.

It also provides the mathematical explanation for the mantra introduced in Session 1:

$$\boxed{\text{Interest Rates}\uparrow \;\Rightarrow\; P_{\text{bond}}\downarrow}$$

When the discount rate increases, the present value of future bond cash flows decreases.

---

# 20. Perpetuities

A **perpetuity** is a stream of equal cash flows that continues forever.

Suppose an investment pays:

$$C$$

every year forever and the relevant discount rate is $r$.

The class introduced the relationship:

$$PV \times r = C$$

Therefore:

$$\boxed{PV = \frac{C}{r}}$$

Example:

If an asset pays €50 every year forever and the discount rate is 5%:

$$PV = \frac{50}{0.05} = 1,000$$

This provides a highly intuitive way to understand the perpetuity formula.

---

# 21. Annuities

An **annuity** is similar to a perpetuity, but the payments continue only for a finite number of periods.

The class introduced a particularly intuitive way of understanding an annuity:

> An annuity can be viewed as the difference between two perpetuities.

The first perpetuity begins immediately.

The second is a delayed perpetuity beginning after the final desired annuity payment.

Conceptually:

$$\text{Annuity} = \text{Perpetuity} - \text{Deferred Perpetuity}$$

This decomposition produces the standard present-value relationship for an ordinary annuity:

$$\boxed{PV_{\text{annuity}} = \frac{C}{r} \left[ 1 - \frac{1}{(1+r)^t} \right]}$$

The main objective was not memorization.

It was understanding **where the formula comes from**.

# 23. What Matters Most from This Session

Students should leave the session comfortable with the following ideas.

### 1. Finance is fundamentally about moving value through time.

$$FV = PV(1+r)^t$$

---

### 2. Compounding makes time extraordinarily powerful.

Starting earlier allows returns to generate additional returns.

---

### 3. Most valuation problems are variations of the same structure.

Usually we know some combination of:

- present value;
    
- future value;
    
- return;
    
- time;
    

and solve for the missing variable.

---

### 4. Multiple cash flows are valued one by one.

$$PV = \sum \frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t}$$

---

### 5. A perpetuity is an infinite stream of equal payments.

$$PV = \frac{C}{r}$$

---

### 6. An annuity is a finite stream of equal payments.

It can be understood as the difference between two perpetuities.

---

### 7. Time Value of Money is the mathematical foundation of bond valuation.

This material will therefore return repeatedly throughout the fixed-income section of the course.

---

# 24. Key Formulas

### Future Value

$$\boxed{FV = PV(1+r)^t}$$

### Present Value

$$\boxed{PV = \frac{FV}{(1+r)^t}}$$

### Rate of Return

$$\boxed{r = \left(\frac{FV}{PV}\right)^{1/t} - 1}$$

### Time

$$\boxed{t = \frac{\log(FV/PV)}{\log(1+r)}}$$

### Multiple Cash Flows

$$\boxed{PV = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t}}$$

### Perpetuity

$$\boxed{PV = \frac{C}{r}}$$

### Ordinary Annuity

$$\boxed{PV = \frac{C}{r} \left[ 1 - \frac{1}{(1+r)^t} \right]}$$

Session 2 introduced one of the most important principles in the entire course:

> **Money has a time value.**

> **What is a future cash flow worth today — or what will today's money be worth in the future?**

That logic will provide the foundation for fixed income, bond pricing, investment valuation and many of the financial models developed during the rest of the course.
