# Financial Markets – Session 16: Derivatives, Futures & Options

## 1. Session Overview

Session 16 opened the final major block of the course:

> **Derivatives**

The class connected derivatives with almost every major idea studied during the semester:

- finance as the management of an unpredictable future;
- financial instruments as contracts;
- risk transfer;
- hedging;
- speculation;
- regulated vs OTC markets;
- liquidity;
- no-arbitrage;
- present value and future value;
- long and short positions.

The core progression was:

$$
Financial\ Instruments \rightarrow Derivatives \rightarrow Futures/Forwards \rightarrow Options \rightarrow Hedging\ and\ Speculation
$$

The central idea was:

> **Derivatives do not allow us to predict the future. They allow us to manage exposure to it.**

---

# PART I — BACK TO THE FOUNDATIONS

## 2. What Is Finance?

The session returned immediately to the definition repeated throughout the course:

$$
\boxed{ Finance = Managing\ an\ Unpredictable\ Future }
$$

We cannot know exactly what will happen.

But we can:

- allocate resources;
- manage risks;
- transfer risks;
- hedge exposures;
- prepare for different scenarios.

This is precisely where derivatives become useful.

---

## 3. What Is the Financial System?

The financial system connects:

$$
Economic\ Agents\ with\ Excess\ Resources
$$

with:

$$
Economic\ Agents\ that\ Need\ Resources
$$

In simplified terms:

$$
Savings \rightarrow Financial\ System \rightarrow Financing\ Needs
$$

Financial instruments are the contracts that make this allocation possible.

---

## 4. What Is a Financial Instrument?

The class again defined a financial instrument as a contract between parties.

From an accounting perspective, it normally creates:

- a financial asset for one party;
- a financial liability or equity instrument for another.

The course organized financial instruments into broad levels.

### Level 1 — Cash

The most liquid form.

### Level 2 — Financing instruments

Examples:

- loans;
- bonds;
- stocks.

These allow firms, governments and individuals to obtain financing.

### Level 3 — Derivatives

Contracts whose value depends on another asset or variable.

---

# PART II — WHY STABILITY MATTERS

## 5. When Finance Works, We Barely Notice It

The class used a simple analogy.

When trains work properly, people do not spend all day discussing the railway system.

They simply travel.

Money works in a similar way.

When confidence is high:

- people accept money;
- transactions occur;
- prices function;
- everyday economic activity continues.

When the system stops working properly, everyone suddenly begins talking about:

- inflation;
- banks;
- liquidity;
- confidence;
- financial crises.

This gives us an important idea:

> **A successful financial system is often almost invisible.**

---

## 6. Confidence Is Part of Financial Infrastructure

Money works because economic agents broadly trust that:

$$
Money\ Today
$$

will still allow them to buy goods and services later.

Confidence therefore underlies:

- currency;
- banking;
- markets;
- financial contracts.

This connects derivatives with previous sessions on banking and systemic risk.

---

# PART III — WHAT WE SEE AND WHAT WE DO NOT SEE

## 7. Survivorship Bias

The class used the famous aircraft-damage example.

During wartime, analysts studied the bullet holes on aircraft that returned from missions.

The initial temptation was to reinforce the places where the returning planes had been hit.

But the deeper insight was:

> The most important areas may be the places where the returning aircraft had **not** been hit.

Why?

Because aircraft hit in those critical areas may never have returned.

This is a classic example of:

$$
\boxed{Survivorship\ Bias}
$$

---

## 8. Application to Financial Crises

The same logic can be applied to finance.

We remember visible crises such as:

- Silicon Valley Bank;
- major bank failures;
- market collapses.

But the financial system may also contain vulnerabilities that have not yet become highly visible.

The lesson is:

> **Do not analyse only what you can see. Ask what may be missing from the sample.**

---

# PART IV — NOISE, INFORMATION & FINANCIAL STABILITY

## 9. Noise vs Signal

The class again used the recent AI/NVIDIA discussion to illustrate how difficult it is to separate:

$$
Signal
$$

from:

$$
Noise
$$

Financial markets are filled with:

- earnings headlines;
- scandals;
- IPOs;
- political stories;
- geopolitical events;
- social-media narratives.

The investor has to determine which information actually changes the underlying financial situation.

---

## 10. Do Not Chase Falling Prices

A practical warning appeared during the discussion:

> **Never try to catch a falling knife.**

A large price fall does not automatically mean an asset is cheap.

If an asset falls from:

$$
200 \rightarrow 180
$$

it may appear cheaper.

If it then falls to:

$$
150
$$

the same argument can be repeated.

Therefore:

$$
Lower\ Price \neq Automatically\ Undervalued
$$

Price and value remain different concepts.

---

# PART V — DERIVATIVES

## 11. What Is a Derivative?

A derivative is a financial instrument whose value depends on an underlying asset or variable.

Conceptually:

$$
\boxed{ Derivative\ Value = f(Underlying) }
$$

Examples of underlying assets may include:

- stocks;
- bonds;
- interest rates;
- currencies;
- commodities;
- indices.

---

## 12. The Underlying Asset

A derivative cannot be properly analysed in isolation.

For example, to understand an option on Apple stock, we need information about:

$$
Apple\ Stock
$$

That is the:

$$
\boxed{Underlying\ Asset}
$$

The derivative derives its value from it.

---

# PART VI — FINANCING INSTRUMENTS VS DERIVATIVES

## 13. A Different Economic Logic

The class contrasted stocks and bonds with derivatives.

When an investor buys a bond or stock, the transaction can contribute to financing productive activity.

Conceptually:

$$
Investor \rightarrow Company
$$

and if the company succeeds:

$$
Company\ Generates\ Value \rightarrow Investor\ Receives\ Return
$$

This can be viewed as a positive-sum economic relationship.

---

## 14. Derivatives and Zero-Sum Payoffs

For many derivative contracts, the payoff between the two counterparties is approximately zero-sum before transaction costs.

If one side gains:

$$
+10
$$

the other side loses:

$$
-10
$$

Therefore:

$$
+10 + (-10)=0
$$

This is fundamentally different from financing a productive asset.

### Technical note

The contractual payoff between counterparties may be zero-sum, but derivatives can still create economic value through:

- hedging;
- risk transfer;
- price discovery;
- lower transaction costs;
- improved allocation of risk.

---

# PART VII — FUTURES AND FORWARDS

## 15. The Basic Future Contract

The class used a simple example.

Suppose today two parties agree that, one year from now:

- one party will deliver a chair;
- the other party will pay €100.

Today:

$$
Contract\ Agreed
$$

In one year:

$$
Payment + Delivery
$$

The key point is:

> The price is agreed today, but the transaction occurs later.

---

## 16. Future Uncertainty

Suppose the agreed contract price is:

$$
K=100
$$

In one year, the market price could be:

$$
80,\ 90,\ 110,\ 120
$$

Nobody knows today.

That uncertainty creates gains for one side and losses for the other.

---

## 17. Long Position in a Future

The buyer agrees to buy at:

$$
K=100
$$

If, at maturity:

$$
S_T=120
$$

the buyer effectively obtains something worth 120 for 100.

Payoff:

$$
120-100=20
$$

If:

$$
S_T=90
$$

the buyer is committed to buy for 100 something worth 90.

Payoff:

$$
90-100=-10
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{ Payoff_{Long\ Future}=S_T-K }
$$

---

## 18. Short Position in a Future

For the seller:

$$
\boxed{ Payoff_{Short\ Future}=K-S_T }
$$

The two positions are exact opposites.

$$
Payoff_{Long} = -Payoff_{Short}
$$

This illustrates the zero-sum nature of the contract.

---

# PART VIII — DO YOU PAY TODAY?

## 19. Futures Are Commitments

One of the important conceptual distinctions was:

> Entering a futures contract is not the same as buying the underlying asset today.

The contract creates a commitment regarding a future transaction.

Conceptually:

$$
Today: Agreement
$$

$$
Future: Settlement
$$

### Technical note

In real organized futures markets, margin is normally required and positions are typically marked to market. The class distinction was focused on the fact that the investor is not paying the full underlying purchase price at contract inception.

---

# PART IX — FUTURES VS FORWARDS

## 20. Two Related Contracts

The class distinguished:

### Futures

Standardized contracts traded on organized markets.

### Forwards

Customized contracts typically traded OTC.

The economic idea is similar:

> Agree today on conditions for a transaction that will occur in the future.

---

## 21. Standardization

The trade-off is:

$$
Standardization \rightarrow Higher\ Liquidity
$$

but also:

$$
Standardization \rightarrow Less\ Flexibility
$$

A forward can be tailored to:

- exact amount;
- exact maturity;
- specific underlying;
- particular needs.

A standardized future is generally easier to trade.

---

## 22. Futures and Market Structure

The session connected derivatives with the earlier discussion of:

$$
Regulated\ Markets
$$

and:

$$
OTC\ Markets
$$

In simplified form:

|Instrument|Typical Market|
|---|---|
|Futures|Organized / regulated market|
|Forwards|OTC|

This shows why understanding market structure was necessary before studying derivatives.

---

# PART X — FUTURES PRICING

## 23. Spot and Future Prices

The class connected futures pricing with Time Value of Money.

For a simple asset with no income or other relevant carrying costs:

$$
F_0=S_0(1+r)^T
$$

where:

- $S_0$: spot price today;
- $F_0$: theoretical future/futures price;
- $r$: financing rate;
- $T$: time.

This should look familiar because:

$$
FV=PV(1+r)^T
$$

The same financial logic appears again.

---

## 24. Futures Do Not Predict the Future

This was one of the strongest messages of the session:

$$
\boxed{ Futures\ Price \neq Prediction\ of\ Future\ Spot\ Price }
$$

A futures price is linked to:

- today's spot price;
- financing costs;
- other carrying costs and benefits;
- no-arbitrage relationships.

It is not a crystal ball.

---

## 25. Spot-Futures Parity

If spot and futures prices become inconsistent, arbitrage opportunities may arise.

Therefore:

$$
Spot \leftrightarrow Futures
$$

are connected through:

$$
\boxed{No\ Arbitrage}
$$

This returns directly to one of the original principles of the course:

> **No-arbitrage keeps prices consistent.**

---

# PART XI — OPTIONS

## 26. Futures vs Options

The crucial difference is:

### Future

Creates an **obligation**.

### Option

Creates a **right** for the buyer.

This distinction is fundamental.

---

## 27. Call Option

A call gives the holder:

$$
\boxed{Right\ to\ Buy}
$$

an underlying asset at a predetermined price.

That predetermined price is the:

$$
\boxed{Strike\ Price}
$$

---

## 28. Put Option

A put gives the holder:

$$
\boxed{Right\ to\ Sell}
$$

the underlying asset at the strike price.

Therefore:

$$
Call=Right\ to\ Buy
$$

$$
Put=Right\ to\ Sell
$$

---

# PART XII — THREE DIFFERENT PRICES

## 29. Underlying Price

The current market price of the asset:

$$
S
$$

---

## 30. Strike Price

The contractual price written into the option:

$$
K
$$

It may also be called:

- exercise price;
- contract price.

---

## 31. Option Premium

The price paid to acquire the option:

$$
\boxed{Premium}
$$

Therefore, when analysing an option, do not confuse:

$$
Underlying\ Price
$$

$$
Strike\ Price
$$

$$
Option\ Premium
$$

They are three different things.

---

# PART XIII — CALL PAYOFF

## 32. Call at Expiration

Suppose:

$$
K=100
$$

If:

$$
S_T=120
$$

the holder can buy at 100 something worth 120.

Payoff:

$$
20
$$

If:

$$
S_T=90
$$

why buy for 100 something available in the market for 90?

The holder simply does not exercise.

Payoff:

$$
0
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{ Call\ Payoff = \max(S_T-K,0) }
$$

---

## 33. Call Profit

Suppose the option premium was:

$$
C=5
$$

Profit becomes:

$$
\boxed{ Call\ Profit = \max(S_T-K,0)-C }
$$

The break-even point is:

$$
K+C
$$

Thus:

$$
100+5=105
$$

---

# PART XIV — PUT PAYOFF

## 34. Put at Expiration

Suppose:

$$
K=100
$$

If:

$$
S_T=90
$$

the holder has the right to sell for 100 something worth only 90.

Payoff:

$$
10
$$

If:

$$
S_T=110
$$

the holder will not exercise the right to sell for 100.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{ Put\ Payoff = \max(K-S_T,0) }
$$

---

## 35. Put Profit

If the put premium is:

$$
P
$$

then:

$$
\boxed{ Put\ Profit = \max(K-S_T,0)-P }
$$

The premium matters when moving from payoff to profit.

---

# PART XV — BUYERS AND SELLERS

## 36. Buying an Option

The buyer:

- pays the premium;
- receives the right;
- has no obligation to exercise.

Maximum loss for the buyer is generally limited to:

$$
\boxed{Premium}
$$

---

## 37. Selling an Option

The seller:

- receives the premium;
- accepts an obligation.

This creates a very different risk profile.

In particular, selling an uncovered call can create very large potential losses if the underlying price rises sharply.

This is why the class stressed that derivatives can be dangerous when used without understanding the risk.

---

# PART XVI — OPTIONS AND INSURANCE

## 38. The Connection with Session 12

The session returned to the insurance analogy.

Buying an option resembles buying insurance.

You:

1. pay a premium;
2. obtain protection or a right;
3. receive a benefit if the relevant event occurs.

Conceptually:

$$
Premium \rightarrow Protection
$$

This applies particularly well to protective puts.

---

## 39. Selling Options as Insurance

Selling an option resembles acting as an insurance company.

The seller receives the premium.

But if the adverse event materializes:

$$
Seller \rightarrow Potentially\ Large\ Payment
$$

Therefore:

> **Receiving a small premium does not imply assuming a small risk.**

---

# PART XVII — HEDGING VS SPECULATION

## 40. Hedging

A derivative can reduce an existing risk.

Example:

An investor owns a stock and buys a put.

$$
Long\ Stock + Long\ Put
$$

The put provides downside protection.

This is:

$$
\boxed{Hedging}
$$

---

## 41. Speculation

A trader may also use derivatives simply to take a view on future price movements.

For example:

- buy a call expecting a rise;
- buy a put expecting a fall.

This is:

$$
\boxed{Speculation}
$$

The same instrument can therefore serve completely different purposes.

---

# PART XVIII — LONG, SHORT, BULLISH & BEARISH

## 42. Bullish Market View

If you expect prices to rise, you are:

$$
\boxed{Bullish}
$$

Possible positions include:

- long the stock;
- long a call.

---

## 43. Bearish Market View

If you expect prices to fall, you are:

$$
\boxed{Bearish}
$$

Possible positions include:

- short the stock;
- long a put.

---

## 44. Long Does Not Always Mean Bullish

This becomes slightly confusing with options.

You can be:

$$
Long\ Put
$$

while holding a bearish market view.

Why?

Because "long" refers to:

> **buying the instrument**

not necessarily expecting the underlying market itself to rise.

Therefore:

$$
Long\ Call \rightarrow Bullish
$$

$$
Long\ Put \rightarrow Bearish
$$

---

# PART XIX — AMERICAN VS EUROPEAN OPTIONS

## 45. Exercise Style

The class introduced two option exercise styles.

### American option

May be exercised at any time up to expiration.

### European option

May only be exercised at expiration.

This has nothing to do with geography.

A Spanish stock can have both American-style and European-style options.

---

## 46. Flexibility Has Value

An American option gives the holder at least as much flexibility as an otherwise equivalent European option.

Therefore, conceptually:

$$
American\ Option\ Value \geq European\ Option\ Value
$$

for otherwise identical contracts.

### Technical note

The statement that early exercise "never" makes sense is too broad. For example, early exercise of some American puts or dividend-paying calls can sometimes be optimal. The class's broader intuition remains useful: an option can possess additional time value before expiration.

---

# PART XX — IN, AT & OUT OF THE MONEY

## 47. Call Option

For a call:

### In the money

$$
S\gt K
$$

### At the money

$$
S\approx K
$$

### Out of the money

$$
S\lt K
$$

---

## 48. Put Option

For a put:

### In the money

$$
S\lt K
$$

### At the money

$$
S\approx K
$$

### Out of the money

$$
S\gt K
$$

These terms describe the relationship between:

$$
Underlying\ Price
$$

and:

$$
Strike\ Price
$$

---

# PART XXI — WHY OPTIONS CAN PRODUCE EXTREME RETURNS

## 49. Nonlinear Payoffs

An option can cost very little when it is deeply out of the money.

Suppose a call costs only a small premium because:

$$
S \ll K
$$

If the underlying price later rises dramatically above the strike, the option value can increase enormously.

This creates:

$$
Small\ Initial\ Premium
$$

with potentially:

$$
Very\ Large\ Percentage\ Return
$$

But the reverse is equally important.

The option can expire worthless.

---

## 50. Risk of Losing 100%

For an option buyer:

$$
Out\ of\ the\ Money\ at\ Expiration
$$

can mean:

$$
Option\ Value=0
$$

Therefore:

$$
Loss=-100\%
$$

of the premium paid.

This is why options should not be confused with ordinary long-term investing.

---

# PART XXII — OPTION PRICING

## 51. Black-Scholes Preview

The class introduced the **Black-Scholes model** as one of the major formulas used to price options.

The detailed model was left for the next session.

The central idea is that option value depends on several variables, including:

- underlying price;
- strike price;
- time to expiration;
- interest rates;
- volatility.

The option price therefore changes continuously as these variables change.

---

## 52. Time Value

An option with time remaining may be worth more than its immediate exercise value.

This explains why:

$$
Option\ Price \neq Only\ Intrinsic\ Value
$$

Before expiration, there is also:

$$
\boxed{Time\ Value}
$$

This will become important when studying option pricing more formally.

---

# PART XXIII — FIVE IDEAS TO LEAVE THE ROOM WITH

## 53. Core Ideas

### 1. Derivatives derive value from an underlying asset

$$
Derivative=f(Underlying)
$$

### 2. Futures create obligations

$$
Long\ Future=S_T-K
$$

### 3. Options create rights

$$
Call=Right\ to\ Buy
$$

$$
Put=Right\ to\ Sell
$$

### 4. Futures prices do not predict the future

They are linked to spot prices through financing costs and no-arbitrage relationships.

### 5. Derivatives can hedge risk or create risk

The instrument itself is neither good nor bad.

Its purpose and position matter.

# Final Takeaway

Session 16 brings the course back to its starting point.

Finance exists because:

$$
\boxed{The\ Future\ Is\ Uncertain}
$$

Bonds help transfer money through time.

Stocks allow investors to participate in business risk.

Insurance transfers particular risks.

Derivatives go one step further:

> **They allow us to design contracts around future states of the world.**

A future fixes a transaction today for execution tomorrow.

An option buys a right without imposing the same obligation on its holder.

Neither instrument predicts what will happen.

They change what happens **to us** depending on what the future turns out to be.

That is the essence of risk management.
