# Financial Markets – Session 15: Geopolitics, IPOs & Equity Markets

## 1. Session Overview

Session 15 formally opened the **equity markets** block.

However, before moving into stocks, the class began with a broader question:

> **What is happening in the world right now, and why should a finance student care?**

The discussion connected:

- historical power structures;
- Yalta and Bretton Woods;
- the shift from the Atlantic to the Pacific;
- current geopolitical meetings;
- NVIDIA and the AI investment cycle;
- information overload;
- IPOs;
- primary and secondary equity markets;
- trading orders;
- takeovers;
- equity valuation;
- dividends.

The underlying structure was:

$$
History
\rightarrow
Geopolitics
\rightarrow
Information
\rightarrow
Markets
\rightarrow
Equity\ Valuation
$$

The central message was again:

> **Finance is not about predicting the future. It is about understanding enough of the system to make decisions under uncertainty.**

---

# PART I — HISTORY AS A FINANCIAL TOOL

## 2. Why Start with Yalta?

The class began by revisiting the **Yalta Conference** at the end of World War II.

The names emphasized were:

- Franklin D. Roosevelt;
- Winston Churchill;
- Joseph Stalin.

The purpose was not simply historical knowledge.

The point was to think about how major powers redesign the international order after periods of profound disruption.

The class used Yalta as a framework for asking:

> Who has power?

> Who is sitting at the table?

> Who is not?

> How is the world being reorganized?

---

## 3. Yalta and Bretton Woods

The historical sequence discussed was:

$$
World\ War\ II
$$

$$
\downarrow
$$

$$
Yalta
$$

$$
\downarrow
$$

$$
Bretton\ Woods
$$

The lesson was that the geopolitical order and the monetary-financial order are connected.

Bretton Woods created a framework for international monetary stability centered on the U.S. dollar.

This links directly with concepts already studied:

- gold;
- currencies;
- international monetary architecture;
- central banks;
- stability.

---

## 4. Stability After Crisis

The broader historical intuition was:

> After extreme economic and political instability, institutions are often designed to prevent another collapse.

The class connected this with:

- hyperinflation;
- war;
- financial instability;
- monetary systems.

Therefore:

$$
Crisis
\rightarrow
Institutional\ Redesign
\rightarrow
Attempted\ Stability
$$

This is one reason financial history remains useful.

---

# PART II — FROM THE ATLANTIC TO THE PACIFIC

## 5. A Changing Centre of Gravity

The class then moved from the post-war Atlantic order to the current Pacific region.

The idea was expressed strongly:

> The world used to revolve predominantly around the Atlantic. Increasingly, the economic and technological centre of gravity is moving toward the Pacific.

This provides a framework for understanding:

- China;
- the United States;
- Russia;
- Asian technology;
- global trade;
- capital flows.

---

## 6. Shenzhen as a Financial and Technological Reference

Shenzhen was introduced as a technological centre close to Hong Kong.

The analogy used was:

$$
Shenzhen
\approx
Chinese\ Silicon\ Valley
$$

The point was not that every Chinese technology company is located there.

The point was that Shenzhen represents the extraordinary concentration of:

- technology;
- manufacturing;
- innovation;
- capital;
- global supply chains.

---

## 7. APEC

The session referred to the ongoing APEC meeting as an example of the shifting global economic map.

The acronym is:

$$
\boxed{
APEC
=
Asia\text{-}Pacific\ Economic\ Cooperation
}
$$

The importance of the discussion was not to memorize diplomatic details.

It was to observe where major economic powers increasingly meet to discuss:

- trade;
- technology;
- capital;
- strategic interests;
- economic stability.

---

## 8. Who Is Sitting at the Table?

The class returned to the Yalta analogy.

The key question was not simply:

> What will political leaders say?

Instead:

> **Which powers are shaping the environment in which future financial decisions will be made?**

This is a financial question because geopolitical decisions can influence:

- tariffs;
- trade;
- currencies;
- energy;
- technology;
- investment;
- interest rates;
- risk premia.

# PART III — FACTS, HYPOTHESES & OPINIONS

## 9. A Necessary Distinction

The professor made an explicit methodological distinction during the session.

There are:

- **facts**;
- **interpretations**;
- **hypotheses**;
- **opinions**.

Students should not confuse them.

A useful analytical structure is:

$$
Fact
\rightarrow
Interpretation
\rightarrow
Hypothesis
\rightarrow
Decision
$$

Each step requires different levels of certainty.

---

## 10. Do Not Tell Students What to Think

The objective of the historical and geopolitical discussion was not to impose a conclusion.

The professor emphasized:

> **I do not want to tell you what to think. I want to give you information so that you can make your own conclusions.**

This is essential in financial analysis.

A market participant should distinguish between:

$$
Evidence
$$

and:

$$
Narrative
$$

## 15. From the Map to the Game

Previous sessions built the financial-system map.

Session 15 moved into what the professor called:

> **the game played on top of the map.**

The institutions are already there.

The supervisors are already there.

Now the question becomes:

> How do equity markets actually work?

# PART VI — PRIMARY AND SECONDARY MARKETS

## 16. Primary Market

The primary market is where a security is issued for the first time.

For stocks, the best-known example is the:

$$
\boxed{IPO}
$$

or:

$$
\boxed{Initial\ Public\ Offering}
$$

In an IPO, shares are offered to investors and the company enters the public equity market.

---

## 17. Secondary Market

After issuance, shares can trade among investors.

This is the:

$$
\boxed{Secondary\ Market}
$$

Conceptually:

### Primary market

$$
Company
\rightarrow
Investor
$$

### Secondary market

$$
Investor
\leftrightarrow
Investor
$$

This distinction applies more broadly than equities, but IPOs make it particularly intuitive.

---

# PART VII — IPOs

## 18. What Happens in an IPO?

An IPO requires substantial preparation.

Financial institutions may help:

- structure the transaction;
- market the company;
- identify investors;
- determine demand;
- support distribution of shares.

Investment banks therefore play a major role.

---

## 19. The Role of Investment Banks

The class discussed institutions such as:

- Goldman Sachs;
- JPMorgan;

as examples of financial intermediaries that can participate in IPO processes.

Their job includes connecting:

$$
Company
$$

with:

$$
Potential\ Investors
$$

The financial institution must generate enough investor demand for the transaction to succeed.

---

## 20. IPO Demand and Initial Price Movements

The class discussed why newly listed shares can experience strong initial demand.

Potential buyers may include:

- institutional investors;
- funds;
- ETFs;
- individual investors.

If demand exceeds the shares immediately available:

$$
Demand \gt  Supply
$$

then:

$$
Price\uparrow
$$

This is basic market mechanics.

---

## 21. Free Float Matters

A company may have a very large theoretical valuation but only a relatively small percentage of shares actively traded.

This is the **free float**.

A low free float can matter because:

$$
Low\ Tradable\ Supply
+
High\ Demand
\rightarrow
Large\ Price\ Movements
$$

Market capitalization alone does not tell the whole story.

---

## 22. Post-IPO Dynamics

A stock can rise strongly immediately after an IPO and later fall.

Reasons can include:

- temporary excess demand;
- changing expectations;
- lock-up expirations;
- insiders eventually selling;
- valuation adjustments.

Therefore:

> A successful first trading day does not automatically mean the company was an attractive long-term investment at that price.

---

# PART VIII — TRADING ORDERS

## 23. Buying a Stock Requires an Order

Suppose an investor wants to buy:

$$
100\ shares
$$

at a specified price.

The investor sends an order through a broker.

This introduces an important practical aspect of equity markets:

> Trading is not simply saying "buy."

Orders contain conditions.

---

## 24. Order Types

The session briefly introduced different types of orders.

Students were not expected to memorize the full catalogue for the final exam.

The important principle was that an investor can specify:

- quantity;
- price;
- execution conditions.

Other orders may be designed to manage risk, such as:

$$
Stop\ Loss
$$

The objective was to understand that execution itself is part of market structure.

---

## 25. Orders and Liquidity

Suppose you want to buy 100 shares.

If only 50 are available at the requested conditions, the outcome depends on the order instructions.

This connects directly with:

$$
\boxed{Liquidity}
$$

A liquid market allows transactions to be executed with limited difficulty and limited price impact.

---

# PART IX — TAKEOVERS

## 26. What Is a Takeover?

A takeover occurs when an investor or company attempts to acquire control of another company.

Conceptually:

$$
Investor
\rightarrow
Large\ Ownership\ Stake
\rightarrow
Control
$$

Control does not necessarily require purchasing every share.

---

## 27. Why Must Large Acquisitions Be Disclosed?

If an investor suddenly attempts to acquire a major portion of a listed company, smaller shareholders may be affected.

This is why regulation may require:

- disclosure;
- public offers;
- specific procedures.

The underlying principle is:

$$
\boxed{
Investor\ Protection
}
$$

Small investors should receive relevant information and appropriate treatment.

---

# PART X — FROM BONDS TO STOCKS

## 28. The Same Valuation Principle

One of the most important moments of the session connected fixed income with equities.

How do we value a bond?

$$
\boxed{
Value = PV(Future\ Cash\ Flows)
}
$$

How do we value a stock?

The same basic idea applies:

$$
\boxed{
Value = PV(Expected\ Future\ Cash\ Flows)
}
$$

The difference lies in the certainty of those cash flows.

---

## 29. Bond Cash Flows

For a standard bond, contractual cash flows are relatively well defined:

- coupons;
- principal repayment.

Therefore:

$$
P_{bond}
=
\sum
\frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t}
$$

The key inputs are known contractually, assuming no default.

---

## 30. Equity Cash Flows

For equities, future cash flows are much more uncertain.

One traditional approach focuses on:

$$
\boxed{Dividends}
$$

Then:

$$
P_0
=
PV(Expected\ Future\ Dividends)
$$

The valuation principle is identical.

The uncertainty is not.

---

# PART XI — DIVIDEND DISCOUNT MODEL

## 31. Basic Idea

If a shareholder ultimately receives value through distributions from the company, a theoretical equity valuation can be based on expected dividends.

Conceptually:

$$
\boxed{
P_0=
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}
\frac{D_t}{(1+k)^t}
}
$$

where:

- $P_0$: current theoretical value;
- $D_t$: expected dividend;
- $k$: required rate of return.

---

## 32. Why Equity Valuation Is Harder

For bonds:

$$
Coupons
$$

are contractually specified.

For equities:

$$
Dividends
$$

are uncertain.

Companies may:

- increase them;
- reduce them;
- suspend them;
- retain earnings instead;
- repurchase shares.

Therefore:

$$
Equity\ Valuation
$$

contains substantially more uncertainty.

---

# PART XII — DIVIDEND ARISTOCRATS

## 33. A Useful Example

The class introduced the concept of **Dividend Aristocrats**.

These are companies with long records of consistently increasing dividends.

The general idea is:

$$
Long\ History
+
Growing\ Dividends
\rightarrow
Greater\ Predictability
$$

relative to firms without a dividend history.

---

## 34. Examples Discussed

Examples mentioned included companies such as:

- Coca-Cola;
- Chevron;
- Caterpillar;
- Clorox;
- IBM;
- Colgate-Palmolive.

The purpose was not to recommend these stocks.

The objective was to illustrate that not all companies have the same cash-flow characteristics.

---

## 35. Different Companies Require Different Analysis

A mature consumer company is not the same as a rapidly growing technology company.

A company with stable dividends may be easier to analyse using dividend-based models.

A high-growth company may:

- reinvest most earnings;
- pay little or no dividend;
- derive much of its value from expected future growth.

Therefore:

$$
Same\ Valuation\ Principle
$$

does not imply:

$$
Same\ Practical\ Model
$$

for every company.

---

# PART XIII — PRICE VS VALUE

## 36. Market Price Is Observable

The market tells us:

$$
\boxed{Price}
$$

every trading day.

---

## 37. Value Must Be Estimated

The analyst must estimate:

$$
\boxed{Value}
$$

based on future cash flows and risk.

This returns to one of the five original ideas of the course:

> **Price is not the same as value.**

---

## 38. Earnings and Prices

A company can publish excellent earnings and still see its stock fall.

Why?

Because the market may have expected even better results.

Similarly, mediocre results can produce a price increase if expectations were worse.

Therefore:

$$
Market\ Reaction
=
Actual\ Information
-
Expected\ Information
$$

This is why financial markets always force us to think about expectations.

That required return can then enter valuation.

# Final Takeaway

Session 15 began with Yalta and ended with dividends.

That is not as strange as it appears.

Yalta was about how power and institutions shape the future.

Bretton Woods was about designing monetary stability.

Current geopolitical meetings are about the rules under which future trade, technology and capital will operate.

An IPO is about financing a company's future.

A stock price is about expectations of future cash flows.

And equity valuation is simply another application of the principle studied since the beginning of the course:

$$
\boxed{
Value
=
Present\ Value\ of\ Expected\ Future\ Cash\ Flows
}
$$

The difficult part is not the formula.

The difficult part is the word:

$$
\boxed{Expected}
$$

That is where uncertainty enters.

And that is why finance exists.
