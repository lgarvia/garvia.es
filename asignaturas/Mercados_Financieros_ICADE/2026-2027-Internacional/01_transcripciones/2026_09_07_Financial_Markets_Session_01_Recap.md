# Financial Markets – Session 1: Introduction & Overview

## 1. Session Overview

The first session introduced the logic, structure, and working philosophy of the course. Rather than beginning with extensive mathematical formalism, the class established a set of basic principles that will recur throughout the semester.

The central idea is that financial markets cannot be understood simply as places where securities are bought and sold. They are systems through which **savings, financing needs, information, risk, liquidity, regulation, expectations, and technology interact**.

Several concepts introduced today will become recurring themes:

- the role of the financial system;
    
- regulated versus less-regulated or decentralized markets;
    
- liquidity;
    
- information;
    
- time and the time value of money;
    
- risk and return;
    
- present value;
    
- no-arbitrage;
    
- leverage and margin;
    
- short selling;
    
- the relationship between interest rates and bond prices.
    

The class also emphasized that finance has to be connected continuously with what is happening in the real world. Monetary policy, fiscal policy, geopolitics, technological change, elections, IPOs, and structural changes in financial markets will therefore be incorporated throughout the course.

---

# 2. The Philosophy of the Course

A fundamental objective of the course is not merely to learn definitions or formulas, but to **understand what is happening in financial markets and why**.

The professor emphasized that students will come into the course with very different backgrounds. Some may already know concepts such as discounted cash flow valuation, present value, leverage, or the cost of capital, whereas others may encounter them for the first time.

This is not a problem.

The course will progressively build the necessary financial intuition.

The main objective is therefore:

> **to learn finance in a way that allows students to interpret real financial events.**

Mathematics and technical tools matter, but they are instruments rather than the final objective.

---

# 3. Finance, Information and Uncertainty

One of the broad themes introduced during the session was the importance of **information**.

Financial markets continuously process information about:

- companies;
    
- interest rates;
    
- central banks;
    
- governments;
    
- elections;
    
- fiscal policy;
    
- geopolitical events;
    
- new technologies;
    
- investor expectations.
    

Financial decisions are therefore fundamentally forward-looking.

The important question is often not simply:

> **What happened?**

but rather:

> **What is going to happen?**

The answer, however, is never perfectly known.

The professor summarized this point very clearly:

> The most important answer is often: **“I don't know.”**

Financial markets exist precisely because the future is uncertain and market participants have different expectations about it.

Federal Reserve policy will be another recurring topic.

- whether rates will rise;

- what the central bank will communicate about future decisions.
    

Prediction markets such as **Polymarket** were mentioned as an example of how market participants can express and trade expectations about future events.

The class also introduced the idea of **forward guidance**: central-bank communication designed to influence expectations regarding future monetary policy.

An important implication is that central-bank communication itself can become financially valuable information.

# 5. What Is a Financial System?

One of the first formal concepts introduced was the financial system.

At its most basic level:

> **The financial system connects savings with financing needs.**

Some economic agents have surplus funds.

Others need financing.

The financial system allows resources to move from one group to the other.

This includes institutions, markets, securities, regulators, central banks, intermediaries, and increasingly technological platforms.

A simplified representation is:

**Savers → Financial System → Borrowers / Investment Opportunities**

This intermediation function is one of the foundations of modern finance.

---

# 6. What Is a Market?

A market should not necessarily be understood as a physical place.

A market is essentially a **platform or mechanism where transactions and information interact**.

Historically, exchanges were strongly associated with physical trading locations.

Technology has dramatically expanded this concept.

Modern markets can be:

- physical;
    
- electronic;
    
- centralized;
    
- decentralized;
    
- regulated;
    
- less regulated;
    
- traditional;
    
- blockchain-based.
    

This makes the boundaries between a financial instrument, a trading platform, and a technological protocol increasingly complex.

---

# 7. Traditional Markets and Digital Markets

The course will distinguish between traditional financial structures and newer technology-based systems.

Cryptocurrency markets were used as an introductory example.

The discussion emphasized that something can operate economically as a market even if it does not fit neatly into the traditional institutional framework of a regulated exchange.

This raises several questions that will appear repeatedly:

- Who supervises the market?
    
- Who guarantees the transaction?
    
- Where does price formation occur?
    
- Who provides liquidity?
    
- What information is available?
    
- What legal framework applies?

# 8. SEC and Market Supervision

The **SEC — U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission** — was introduced as the principal U.S. securities-market regulator.

The broader point was not to study regulation in detail yet, but to introduce an important distinction:

> **economic activity can exist even when its regulatory perimeter is unclear or different from that of traditional markets.**

Cryptocurrency markets provide an obvious example.

The course will later dedicate more attention to financial-market supervision and regulation.

# 9. OTC — Over the Counter

One of the first pieces of financial vocabulary introduced was **OTC**.

OTC stands for:

> **Over the Counter**

The expression was initially explained through the everyday analogy of medicine that can be purchased without a prescription.

In finance, however, OTC refers to transactions that take place **outside a centralized organized exchange**, typically through bilateral or dealer-based arrangements.

The concept will become relevant when comparing:

- organized exchanges;
    
- dealer markets;
    
- bilateral transactions;
    
- derivatives markets;
    
- decentralized or digital markets.
    

The growth and importance of OTC structures was highlighted as something students should keep in mind during the course.

# 10. Liquidity

Another major concept introduced was **liquidity**.

Liquidity will be one of the recurring themes of the semester.

At an intuitive level, liquidity refers to the ability to buy or sell an asset relatively easily.

Liquidity is connected with several other characteristics of markets, including:

- standardization;
    
- trading activity;
    
- availability of counterparties;
    
- market depth;
    
- transaction mechanisms.
    

The course will develop the concept in greater detail later.

For now, students should remember:

> **Liquidity determines how easily financial positions can be transformed or traded.**

# 12. Japan and the Global Financial System

Japan was singled out as particularly important for understanding present financial conditions.

The class referred to the sharp rise in long-term Japanese government bond yields and connected this development with the **carry trade**.

For many years, extremely low Japanese interest rates allowed investors to obtain cheap financing in yen and deploy the funds elsewhere.

This is one reason why changes in Japanese interest rates can have implications far beyond Japan.

The topic will be developed later in the semester.

The general lesson is:

> **A change in one major financial market can affect capital flows across the entire global financial system.**

To understand present-day markets, students need some knowledge of:

- previous financial crises;

- institutional development;
    
- market structures;

The course will therefore use financial history as a tool for understanding current and future developments.

# 14. Time Value of Money

The mathematical foundation introduced in the session was the relationship between present value and future value.

The basic formula is:

PV=FV1+rPV=\frac{FV}{1+r}

where:

- PVPV = Present Value;
    
- FVFV = Future Value;
    
- rr = interest or discount rate.
    

This formula will recur throughout the course.

Its economic meaning is more important than the algebra:

> **Money available at different moments in time does not have the same value.**

This principle will be developed formally in the next session.

---

# 15. Discounted Cash Flow

The concept of **Discounted Cash Flow (DCF)** valuation was briefly introduced.

The transcript occasionally renders the acronym as “VCF,” but the concept discussed in class is the valuation of future cash flows by discounting them to present value.

The basic logic is:

1. estimate future cash flows;
    
2. determine the appropriate discount rate;
    
3. calculate the present value of those cash flows.
    

Conceptually:

Value=∑t=1nCFt(1+r)tValue=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t}

DCF is one of the fundamental approaches used to value businesses and financial assets.

# 16. The Discount Rate and WACC

The session briefly introduced the **Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)**.

When valuing a company through discounted cash flows, the choice of discount rate is crucial.

For enterprise-level cash flows, WACC is commonly used as the relevant discount rate.

The professor also mentioned **CAPM** as one of the concepts that will eventually help students understand the cost of capital.

These topics were only previewed and will be developed later.

# 17. Three Basic Financial Principles

The course introduced several simple axioms that provide the foundations for many later financial models.

## 17.1 More Is Preferred to Less

If an investor can receive:

- €100, or
    
- €200,
    

under otherwise identical conditions, the investor prefers €200.

This apparently obvious statement becomes a useful building block for financial theory.

---

## 17.2 Sooner Is Preferred to Later

If an investor can receive the same amount of money:

- today, or
    
- later,
    

the investor normally prefers receiving it sooner.

This is directly connected with the **time value of money**.

---

## 17.3 Risk Matters

Suppose an investor can choose between:

- €100 with certainty; or
    
- a risky payoff that can produce different outcomes.
    

The decision depends on the characteristics of the risky investment and the investor's attitude toward risk.

This introduces the relationship between:

> **risk and expected return**

which will become one of the central ideas of the course.

---

# 18. Risk and Return

Risk and return are inseparable.

As introduced in class:

> **higher risk normally requires higher expected return.**

An investor will normally demand compensation for accepting additional uncertainty.

This creates one of the fundamental relationships in finance:

Higher Risk→Higher Required Expected ReturnHigher\ Risk \rightarrow Higher\ Required\ Expected\ Return

Risk, however, does not guarantee a higher realized return.

Rather, investors require higher expected compensation for bearing greater risk.

---

# 19. No-Arbitrage

Another central axiom introduced was the absence of arbitrage opportunities.

An **arbitrage opportunity** exists when an investor can construct transactions that generate a profit without corresponding economic risk or investment.

The intuitive example used in class involved a **Happy Meal**.

If the complete package could be bought for less than the combined value of its components, an investor could theoretically:

1. buy the package;
    
2. separate the components;
    
3. sell the components individually;
    
4. earn a profit from the price inconsistency.
    

In competitive markets, traders would exploit the discrepancy.

Their actions would tend to eliminate it.

---

# 20. Arbitrage Through Borrowing and Investing

A second example illustrated the same idea through interest rates.

Suppose an investor can:

- borrow money at **5%**;
    
- invest the same money at a guaranteed **8%**.
    

The investor could:

1. borrow at 5%;
    
2. invest at 8%;
    
3. earn the difference.
    

Conceptually:

8%−5%=3%8\%-5\%=3\%

If both operations are genuinely equivalent in risk and timing, this would represent an arbitrage opportunity.

This principle will become extremely important for pricing financial assets.

---

# 21. A Core Course Mantra: Rates Up, Bond Prices Down

One relationship was explicitly identified as something students should internalize permanently:

> **If interest rates go up, bond prices go down.**

And conversely:

> **If interest rates go down, bond prices go up.**

This inverse relationship will appear repeatedly throughout the course and is likely to be relevant in both the midterm and final examination.

The intuition will be developed later through present-value mathematics.

For now:

r↑⇒Pbond↓r \uparrow \quad \Rightarrow \quad P_{bond} \downarrow r↓⇒Pbond↑r \downarrow \quad \Rightarrow \quad P_{bond} \uparrow

# 22. Investing on Margin

The class briefly introduced **margin trading**.

When buying on margin, an investor does not finance the entire investment using personal capital.

Part of the purchase is financed by borrowing from a broker or financial intermediary.

This introduces **leverage**.

For example:

Investment=Investor Equity+Borrowed FundsInvestment = Investor\ Equity + Borrowed\ Funds

Leverage magnifies the impact of price changes on the investor's own capital.

If the asset price rises, the percentage return on the investor's equity can increase substantially.

However, the opposite is also true.

If the asset price falls, losses are amplified.

Therefore:

> **Leverage magnifies both gains and losses.**

---

# 23. Short Selling

The class also introduced **short selling**.

Normally, an investor:

1. buys an asset;
    
2. waits for its price to rise;
    
3. sells it at a higher price.
    

A short seller reverses the logic.

The investor attempts to profit from a decline in the asset price.

In simplified terms:

1. sell the asset;
    
2. later buy it back;
    
3. profit if the repurchase price is lower than the original selling price.
    

The class referred to **Volkswagen** and **GameStop** as examples related to short selling and unusual market dynamics.

These cases can be explored later when discussing short squeezes, market structure, and leverage.

---

# 24. Long and Short Positions

Students should begin distinguishing between two fundamental market positions.

### Long position

An investor benefits when the asset price rises.

Price↑⇒ProfitPrice\uparrow \Rightarrow Profit

### Short position

An investor benefits when the asset price falls.

Price↓⇒ProfitPrice\downarrow \Rightarrow Profit

This distinction is essential for understanding financial markets, derivatives, hedge funds, and trading strategies.

---

# 25. Leverage on Both Sides of the Market

Leverage can be associated with both:

- long positions;
    
- short positions.
    

This means investors can amplify exposure to either:

- rising prices; or
    
- falling prices.
    

Because leverage amplifies the effect of market movements, it is simultaneously:

- a return-enhancing mechanism;
    
- a source of financial risk.
    

The topic will be revisited in greater detail later.

---

# 26. Markets as Information Mechanisms

An interesting broader idea emerged from the discussion of prediction markets.

A market does not simply transfer assets.

It can also aggregate **information and beliefs**.

Prediction markets such as Polymarket allow participants to place economic value on different future outcomes.

The resulting market prices can therefore be interpreted as a collective measure of expectations.

This provides an important bridge between:

- information;
    
- expectations;
    
- probabilities;
    
- market prices.

- liquidity provision;
    
- market-making mechanisms;
    
- settlement infrastructure.
    

This is one reason why modern financial markets increasingly require students to understand both **finance and technology**.

# Final Takeaway

The first session established the intellectual map for the semester.

Financial markets should not be seen as an isolated collection of products or formulas. They form a global system through which **money, information, expectations, risk and time are priced**.
