# Financial Markets – Session 9: Banking Business, Bank Runs & Financial Stability

## 1. Session Overview

Session 9 opened a new major block of the course:

> **Banking Business**

The central questions were:

- What makes a bank different from any other company that lends money?
- Why are banks so heavily regulated?
- Why is confidence essential to the banking system?
- What is a bank run?
- Why can the failure of one bank become a systemic problem?
- What role do deposit insurance and central banks play in financial stability?

The session also included a substantial review of the material covered before the midterm and clarified the exam structure.

---

# PART I — WHAT IS A BANK?

## 2. Lending Money Does Not Define a Bank

The class began with a simple question:

> **What is a bank?**

A first possible answer was:

> A place where you can borrow money.

But lending money does not uniquely define a bank.

Individuals, companies and many financial institutions can lend money.

For example:

$$

Person \rightarrow Loan \rightarrow Person
$$

does not create a bank.

The distinctive activity is different.

---

## 3. Banks Can Take Deposits from the Public

The key characteristic introduced in class was:

> **Banks can accept deposits from the public.**

This differentiates banks from many other financial institutions.

A normal company cannot simply announce:

> “Give me your savings and I will hold them for you.”

Taking deposits from the general public is a regulated activity.

Therefore:

$$

\boxed{
Banking\ Business
\neq
Simply\ Lending
}
$$

Instead:

$$

\boxed{
Banking\ Business
=
Deposits
+
Loans
+
Financial\ Intermediation
}
$$

The ability to accept deposits is one reason banks require authorization and supervision.

---

## 4. The Taxi Analogy

The class used taxis as an analogy.

A taxi and a ride-hailing service may provide similar transportation services.

However, taxis traditionally operate under a specific public license and regulatory framework.

Similarly, banks are normally private companies, but they perform an activity considered essential to the functioning of society.

Therefore:

> **A private company can provide a service with strong public-interest implications.**

This helps explain why banks are subject to:

- licenses;
- regulation;
- supervision;
- capital requirements;
- liquidity requirements;
- deposit protection.

Banks are private institutions, but banking is not an ordinary private business.

---

# PART II — BANKING AND TRUST

## 5. Banking Is Built on Confidence

A recurring theme from the previous monetary-policy sessions returned:

> **Money is based on trust.**

Banking is also based on trust.

Depositors normally do not think continuously about whether their money is physically available.

They assume:

$$

Deposit\ Today
\rightarrow
Money\ Available\ Tomorrow
$$

This expectation allows the banking system to work efficiently.

The problem appears when confidence disappears.

---

## 6. Why Banks Are Vulnerable

Imagine that you have accumulated your savings over many years.

You leave them in a bank because you believe they are safe.

Then you hear:

> The bank may not be able to return deposits.

Your incentives change immediately.

You may decide:

$$

Withdraw\ Now
>
Wait
$$

But every other depositor may make exactly the same decision.

This creates a **bank run**.

---

# PART III — BANK RUNS

## 7. What Is a Bank Run?

A bank run occurs when many depositors attempt to withdraw their money at the same time because they fear the institution may fail.

Conceptually:

$$

Loss\ of\ Confidence
\rightarrow
Withdrawals
\rightarrow
Liquidity\ Pressure
\rightarrow
More\ Fear
\rightarrow
More\ Withdrawals
$$

This creates a self-reinforcing process.

A bank may therefore experience severe problems even before every underlying asset has lost value.

---

## 8. The Fundamental Liquidity Problem

Banks do not normally keep all deposits permanently in cash.

They use part of those resources to finance:

- mortgages;
- corporate loans;
- government securities;
- other investments.

A simplified balance sheet might look like:

### Assets

- Loans
- Bonds
- Cash

### Liabilities

- Deposits
- Other funding

Depositors can frequently demand their money quickly.

But many bank assets may mature years later.

This produces a fundamental mismatch:

$$

\text{Short-Term Liabilities}
$$

fund:

$$

\text{Long-Term Assets}
$$

This is one of the central characteristics of banking.

---

## 9. Liquidity Risk

This leads directly to **liquidity risk**.

Liquidity risk is the risk that an institution cannot obtain enough cash to meet its immediate obligations without suffering substantial losses.

A bank may own valuable assets but still experience liquidity problems if those assets cannot be converted into cash quickly enough.

Therefore:

$$

Solvency
\neq
Liquidity
$$

A bank can have valuable long-term assets while still facing an immediate liquidity crisis.

---

## 10. The Mary Poppins Example

The class used *Mary Poppins* as a visual illustration of a bank run.

The important lesson is not the specific film scene.

The important mechanism is:

1. confidence disappears;
2. one depositor asks for money;
3. others become nervous;
4. withdrawals spread;
5. liquidity pressure accelerates.

A seemingly small event can therefore produce a disproportionate systemic effect.

This was connected with the idea of the **butterfly effect**.

---

# PART IV — WHY BANK RUNS CAN BECOME SYSTEMIC

## 11. From One Bank to the Whole System

A bank run may initially affect only one institution.

But banking systems are interconnected.

Depositors may start thinking:

> If that bank has problems, perhaps mine does too.

This creates **contagion**.

$$

Bank\ A\ Problem
\rightarrow
Fear
\rightarrow
Bank\ B\ Withdrawals
\rightarrow
Bank\ C\ Withdrawals
$$

A local liquidity problem can therefore become a systemic financial crisis.

---

## 12. Confidence Can Move Faster Than Fundamentals

Banking crises are particularly dangerous because confidence can disappear extremely quickly.

Modern communication makes this process potentially even faster.

Information can now spread almost instantly through:

- social media;
- messaging platforms;
- online banking;
- financial news.

Depositors can also move money without physically going to a branch.

This means:

$$

Information\ Speed\uparrow
\Rightarrow
Potential\ Bank\ Run\ Speed\uparrow
$$

The class connected this idea with the Silicon Valley Bank crisis discussed in previous sessions.

---

# PART V — FINANCIAL HISTORY

## 13. Bank Runs Are Not New

The class reviewed examples of banking crises across history.

The objective was not to memorize every historical episode.

Instead, the main lesson was:

> **Bank runs repeatedly appear when confidence in financial institutions breaks down.**

Different centuries have:

- different technologies;
- different financial instruments;
- different central banks;

but the fundamental problem of trust remains surprisingly similar.

---

## 14. The 1929 Crisis and the Great Depression

The Great Depression was used again as an important reference point.

The class distinguished between:

- a financial crisis;
- the broader economic consequences that can follow.

The central argument discussed was that inadequate policy responses can allow financial-sector problems to spread throughout the real economy.

Conceptually:

$$

Banking\ Crisis
\rightarrow
Credit\ Contraction
\rightarrow
Lower\ Investment
\rightarrow
Lower\ Consumption
\rightarrow
Economic\ Depression
$$

This historical experience became extremely important for later central bankers.

---

# PART VI — BERNANKE, DIAMOND AND DYBVIG

## 15. The 2022 Economics Nobel

The session discussed the work of:

- Ben Bernanke;
- Douglas Diamond;
- Philip Dybvig.

Their research helped explain the role of banks in the economy and why banking crises can become so destructive.

The connection with the course is direct:

> **Why do banks exist, and why are they inherently vulnerable to runs?**

---

## 16. Diamond–Dybvig Intuition

Banks perform an economically valuable transformation.

Depositors want:

- safety;
- liquidity;
- access to money when required.

Borrowers often need:

- long-term financing;
- mortgages;
- business loans.

Banks connect these two groups.

Conceptually:

$$

Short\text{-}Term\ Deposits
\rightarrow
Bank
\rightarrow
Long\text{-}Term\ Loans
$$

This transformation is socially useful.

But it creates vulnerability.

If everyone asks for deposits simultaneously, the bank cannot immediately liquidate every long-term investment without consequences.

---

## 17. Why Deposit Insurance Matters

One mechanism for reducing bank-run incentives is **deposit insurance**.

Suppose a depositor believes:

> Even if my bank fails, my insured deposit will be protected.

Then the incentive to run to the bank at the first sign of uncertainty becomes smaller.

Conceptually:

$$

Deposit\ Insurance
\rightarrow
More\ Confidence
\rightarrow
Lower\ Incentive\ to\ Run
$$

Deposit insurance therefore helps stabilize the system by changing depositor behaviour.

---

## 18. Deposit Insurance Is About Expectations

The important mechanism is psychological as well as financial.

If depositors trust the guarantee, many of them have no reason to rush to withdraw funds.

That can prevent the bank run from occurring in the first place.

This is another example of the recurring theme of the course:

$$

Expectations
\rightarrow
Behaviour
\rightarrow
Financial\ Outcomes
$$

---

## 19. Bernanke and the Lessons of the Great Depression

Ben Bernanke studied the Great Depression extensively before later becoming Chair of the Federal Reserve.

The class connected this research with the response to the 2008 financial crisis.

The lesson presented was:

> If financial institutions fail and policymakers allow the banking system to collapse, the financial crisis can become a much deeper economic crisis.

During the 2008 crisis, authorities therefore intervened aggressively after the collapse of Lehman Brothers to prevent further failures from destabilizing the entire financial system.

This connects directly with the monetary-base expansion studied in Sessions 6 and 7.

---

# PART VII — BANKING CRISES AND SILICON VALLEY BANK

## 20. Revisiting Silicon Valley Bank

Silicon Valley Bank remains a useful case because it combines several concepts already studied:

- interest-rate risk;
- liquidity risk;
- depositor behaviour;
- confidence;
- central-bank intervention.

The sequence can be summarized as:

$$

\text{Interest Rates}\uparrow
$$

$$

\text{Bond Prices}\downarrow
$$

$$

\text{Asset Values}\downarrow
$$

At the same time:

$$

\text{Depositors Withdraw}
$$

The bank may then need to sell securities whose market value has fallen.

This can convert unrealized losses into realized losses and further damage confidence.

---

## 21. Interest-Rate Risk vs Liquidity Risk

Students should clearly distinguish the two.

### Interest-Rate Risk

$$

Rates\uparrow
\Rightarrow
\text{Bond Prices}\downarrow
$$

### Liquidity Risk

$$

Depositors\ Demand\ Cash
>
Immediately\ Available\ Cash
$$

The two risks can reinforce each other.

A bank experiencing withdrawals may be forced to sell bonds precisely when their market prices have fallen.

This is why the Silicon Valley Bank case connects the fixed-income chapter with the banking chapter.

---

# PART VIII — MONEY, BANKS AND THE FINANCIAL SYSTEM

## 22. Banks Are Part of the Monetary System

The banking chapter also connects directly with the previous discussion of money.

We previously distinguished:

### Monetary Base

Central-bank money.

### Money Supply

The broader quantity of money circulating through the economy.

Commercial banks play an essential role in connecting the two.

Banks:

- receive deposits;
- provide loans;
- facilitate payments;
- transform maturities;
- provide liquidity.

They therefore occupy a central position between monetary policy and economic activity.

---

## 23. The Financial System Is a Chain of Trust

The session repeatedly returned to confidence.

People study today because they trust that education will provide future opportunities.

People accept money because they trust that others will accept it.

People keep deposits in banks because they trust that the deposits will remain available.

Financial systems are therefore built on interconnected expectations.

$$

Trust
\rightarrow
Cooperation
\rightarrow
Financial\ Activity
$$

When trust begins to disappear:

$$

Distrust
\rightarrow
Defensive\ Behaviour
\rightarrow
Financial\ Stress
$$

At the extreme:

$$

Loss\ of\ Trust
\rightarrow
Bank\ Run
$$

---

# PART IX — A BRIEF METHODOLOGICAL DIGRESSION

## 24. Natural Experiments

The class briefly discussed the idea of **natural experiments**.

Instead of creating a controlled laboratory experiment, economists can sometimes compare situations produced naturally by history, institutions or policy.

The general logic is:

> Observe similar groups or economies exposed to different conditions and study how their outcomes diverge.

The example of North and South Korea was used to illustrate how historical developments can create dramatically different institutional environments.

The broader methodological lesson was:

> **Economics also learns by observing what happens in the real world.**

# Final Takeaway

Session 9 introduced banking through the simplest possible question:

> **What makes a bank a bank?**

The answer is not simply that banks lend money.

Their distinctive role comes from accepting deposits from the public and transforming those short-term liabilities into longer-term assets.

That transformation is economically valuable.

But it also creates vulnerability.

If confidence disappears, depositors may demand their money simultaneously.

That is why banks need regulation, liquidity support, deposit insurance and a central bank capable of protecting the stability of the financial system.

The central idea is simple:

$$

\boxed{
Banking\ is\ built\ on\ trust
}
$$

When trust works, the financial system becomes almost invisible.

When trust disappears, everything else becomes secondary.
