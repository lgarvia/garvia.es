\boxed{

> **What does it mean for a market to be efficient?**

> **What happens when prices become inconsistent?**

## 2. Finance, Information and Learning

The session began with an analogy.

The important question is not:

> Are people perfect or are they a disaster?

People make mistakes.

But mistakes generate:

$$
\boxed{
\text{text{Learning}}
}
$$
and learning can improve future decisions.

The same intuition was applied to markets.

The important question is not simply:

> **Are markets perfectly efficient?**

Instead:

> **What happens when an inefficiency appears?**

The answer is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{text{Arbitrage}}
}
$$
Market participants identify inconsistencies, trade against them and, through their actions, push prices toward greater efficiency.

---

## 3. What Is an Efficient Market?

The class returned to Eugene Fama's classic definition:

> A market is efficient when prices fully reflect available information.

Conceptually:

$$
\boxed{
Efficient\ Market  
\Rightarrow  
Prices\ Reflect\ Available\ Information
}
$$
Information is therefore the central concept behind market efficiency.

If relevant information is already embedded in prices, investors cannot systematically use that same information to obtain abnormal returns.

This leads to the next question:

> **What exactly do we mean by information?**

---

## 4. Three Forms of Market Efficiency

The Efficient Market Hypothesis distinguishes three information sets:

|Form|Information reflected in prices|
|---|---|
|Weak|Historical prices and trading information|
|Semi-strong|All publicly available information|
|Strong|All information, public and private|

Each version asks whether investors can exploit a particular type of information to earn abnormal returns.

---

## 5. Weak-Form Efficiency

Under weak-form efficiency:

$$
\boxed{
Prices\ Reflect\ Historical\ Market\ Information
}
$$
Examples include:

- past prices;
    
- past returns;
    
- trading patterns;
    
- historical volume.
    

The natural counterpart is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{text{Technical Analysis}}
}
$$
Technical analysis attempts to predict future prices from patterns contained in historical market data.

If markets were perfectly weak-form efficient:

$$
\boxed{
Historical\ Prices  
\not\Rightarrow  
Systematic\ Abnormal\ Profits
}
$$
Any easily exploitable historical pattern should attract traders and eventually disappear.

---

## 6. Technical Analysis and Learning

The class used candlestick charts and trading patterns to illustrate technical analysis.

A trader may identify:

- trends;
    
- support or resistance levels;
    
- price formations;
    
- recurring patterns.
    

If a pattern genuinely produces predictable profits, investors will begin exploiting it.

That trading changes prices.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
Profitable\ Pattern  
\rightarrow  
Exploitation  
\rightarrow  
Pattern\ Weakens
}
$$
This is an important feature of financial markets:

> **Discovering an inefficiency can contribute to destroying the inefficiency.**

---

## 7. Semi-Strong-Form Efficiency

Semi-strong efficiency expands the information set.

Prices would reflect not only historical prices but also:

$$
\boxed{
\text{text{All Public Information}}
}
$$
Examples include:

- earnings announcements;
    
- balance sheets;
    
- annual reports;
    
- stock splits;
    
- company news;
    
- economic information;
    
- publicly available corporate information.
    

The relevant investment activity here is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{text{Fundamental Analysis}}
}
$$
---

## 8. Fundamental Analysis and Value Investing

Fundamental analysis attempts to estimate the intrinsic value of a company using information such as:

- earnings;
    
- cash flows;
    
- assets;
    
- growth;
    
- financial statements;
    
- competitive position.
    

The logic is familiar from earlier sessions:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Intrinsic Value} = \text{PV}(\text{Future Cash Flows})
}
$$

The investor compares:

$$
\text{Intrinsic  Value}
$$
with:

$$
\text{Market  Price}
$$
If:

$$
\text{Intrinsic Value} \gt \text{Market Price}
$$
the stock may appear undervalued.

This is the basic intuition behind **value investing**.

---

## 9. Strong-Form Efficiency

Strong-form efficiency uses the broadest possible information set:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Public  Information  
+  
Private  Information}
}
$$
If markets were perfectly strong-form efficient, even investors possessing private information would not systematically obtain abnormal returns.

In reality, private information can clearly have value.

This is why financial markets regulate:

$$
\boxed{
\text{text{Insider Trading}}
}
$$
Trading on material non-public information can be illegal.

---

## 10. Information and Price Reactions

The class discussed how regulators can examine price movements around corporate announcements.

Suppose important information becomes public at time:

$$
t=0
$$
If prices move substantially **before** the public announcement, this may indicate that some market participants possessed the information beforehand.

Therefore event timing can provide evidence about:

- information leakage;
    
- insider trading;
    
- how quickly information enters market prices.
    

This connects market efficiency directly with real-world market supervision.

---

## 11. Market Anomalies

An **anomaly** is an empirical pattern that appears inconsistent with a simple version of market efficiency or with a particular pricing model.

Examples can involve recurring relationships between:

- calendar dates;
    
- company characteristics;
    
- historical returns;
    
- valuation ratios;
    
- future performance.
    

But there is an important paradox.

Once an anomaly becomes widely known:

1. Researchers publish it.
    
2. Investors attempt to exploit it.
    
3. Trading changes prices.
    
4. The anomaly may weaken or disappear.
    

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
Discovered\ Anomaly  
\rightarrow  
Arbitrage  
\rightarrow  
Greater\ Efficiency
}
$$
---

## 12. A Warning About CAPM

The class also connected market efficiency with CAPM.

Suppose observed returns do not fit CAPM perfectly.

It does **not automatically follow** that markets are inefficient.

Another possibility is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{text{The model is incomplete}}
}
$$
CAPM is a powerful framework, but it is still a model.

Its simplicity comes from reducing relevant risk largely to:

$$
\boxed{
\text{beta}
}
$$
Other company characteristics may also help explain differences in returns.

The methodological lesson was:

> **Do not confuse failure of a model with failure of the market.**

Or, in the analogy used in class:

> **If all you have is a hammer, everything starts to look like a nail.**

CAPM is useful, but it should not be applied mechanically to every problem.

---

## 13. Market Efficiency and Arbitrage

The second half of the session moved directly to arbitrage.

The central statement was:

$$
\boxed{
Efficient\ Market  
\Longleftrightarrow  
No\ Exploitable\ Arbitrage
}
$$
Arbitrage means constructing a strategy that exploits inconsistent prices.

In its pure theoretical form, arbitrage generates:

- no net future risk;
    
- no net investment, or an immediate positive cash flow;
    
- a positive payoff.
    

The possibility of arbitrage creates an incentive for traders to correct inconsistent prices.

---

## 14. Law of One Price

The first principle introduced was the:

$$
\boxed{
\text{text{Law of One Price}}
}
$$
If two securities produce exactly the same future payoffs under the same conditions, they should have the same price today.

Formally:

$$
\boxed{
Same\ Future\ Cash\ Flows  
\Rightarrow  
Same\ Price\ Today
}
$$
If their prices differ, an arbitrage opportunity exists.

---

## 15. CATS and TIGRs Example

The class considered two securities that both promise:

$$
\text{\$100}
$$
one year from today.

Suppose:

$$
P_{CATS}=94.34
$$
and:

$$
P_{TIGRs}=95.24
$$
Both securities have the same future payoff.

But TIGRs are more expensive.

An arbitrageur can:

1. Buy the cheaper security: CATS.
    
2. Short the more expensive security: TIGRs.
    

Today:

$$
CF_0=-94.34+95.24
$$
$$
\boxed{
CF_0=+0.90
}
$$
In one year:

- CATS pays (+100).
    
- The short TIGRs position requires (-100).
    

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
CF_1=0
}
$$
The arbitrageur receives:

$$
\boxed{
\text{\$0.90}
}
$$
today with offsetting future obligations.

---

## 16. How Arbitrage Corrects Prices

Once investors discover this opportunity:

- demand for CATS increases;
    
- investors short or sell TIGRs.
    

Therefore:

$$
\text{P_{CATS} uparrow}
$$
and:

$$
\text{P_{TIGRs} downarrow}
$$
The price difference narrows.

Eventually:

$$
\boxed{
P_{CATS}\approx P_{TIGRs}
}
$$
and the arbitrage opportunity disappears.

This is the mechanism through which arbitrage contributes to market efficiency.

---

## 17. Transaction Costs

Pure arbitrage arguments often assume frictionless markets.

Real markets contain:

- commissions;
    
- bid-ask spreads;
    
- borrowing costs;
    
- short-selling costs;
    
- taxes;
    
- execution costs.
    

Suppose the apparent arbitrage profit is:

$$
\text{\$0.90}
$$
but executing the strategy costs:

$$
\text{\$1.00}
$$
Then the trade is no longer profitable.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
Transaction\ Costs  
\Rightarrow  
A\ Band\ of\ Possible\ Prices
}
$$
Small price differences may persist because exploiting them costs more than the potential profit.

Thus transaction costs can make real markets less perfectly efficient than theoretical frictionless markets.

---

## 18. Replicating Portfolios

The final exercise introduced an even more powerful no-arbitrage technique:

$$
\boxed{
\text{text{Replication}}
}
$$
If a portfolio of existing securities reproduces exactly the future cash flows of another security, both must have the same value today.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Same Cash Flows} \implies \text{Same Present Value}
}
$$
This is one of the most important ideas in modern finance.

---

## 19. Zero-Coupon Bond Example

Suppose three zero-coupon bonds have face value:

$$
100
$$
and current prices:

|Maturity|Price|
|---|---|
|1 year|98|
|2 years|96|
|3 years|93|

Now consider a three-year bond with:

- Face value: \$100.
    
- Annual coupon rate: 10%.
    

Its cash flows are:

|Year|Cash Flow|
|---|---|
|1|10|
|2|10|
|3|110|

The question is:

> **What must this coupon bond be worth if arbitrage is impossible?**

---

## 20. Constructing the Replicating Portfolio

We can reproduce the coupon bond's cash flows using the zero-coupon bonds.

### Year 1

To obtain:

$$
10
$$
buy:

$$
10%
$$
of the one-year zero.

Cost:

$$
0.10(98)=9.8
$$
### Year 2

To obtain:

$$
10
$$
buy:

$$
10%
$$
of the two-year zero.

Cost:

$$
0.10(96)=9.6
$$
### Year 3

To obtain:

$$
110
$$
buy:

$$
110%
$$
of the three-year zero.

Cost:

$$
1.10(93)=102.3
$$
Total cost:

$$
9.8+9.6+102.3
$$
$$
\boxed{
P=121.7
}
$$
Therefore the coupon bond must be worth:

$$
\boxed{
121.7
}
$$
if arbitrage is impossible.

---

## 21. Same Answer Through Discounting

The same price can be obtained by calculating the spot rate implied by each zero-coupon bond and discounting each coupon bond cash flow separately.

Conceptually:

$$
P=  
\frac{10}{1+r_1}  
+  
\frac{10}{(1+r_2)^2}  
+  
\frac{110}{(1+r_3)^3}
$$
This produces the same value:

$$
\boxed{
P=121.7
}
$$
Why?

Because both methods are based on exactly the same principle:

$$
\boxed{
\text{text{No Arbitrage}}
}
$$
The coupon bond and the replicating portfolio produce identical future cash flows.

Therefore they must have identical prices today.

---

## 22. What If the Coupon Bond Trades at \$100?

Suppose the replicating portfolio costs:

$$
121.7
$$
but the coupon bond trades at:

$$
100
$$
The coupon bond is cheaper even though both strategies generate the same future cash flows.

An arbitrageur could:

1. Buy the coupon bond for (100).
    
2. Short the replicating portfolio for (121.7).
    

Immediate cash flow:

$$
121.7-100
$$
$$
\boxed{
+21.7
}
$$
Future cash flows cancel because the two positions produce opposite identical payments.

Again:

$$
\boxed{
Price\ Difference  
\rightarrow  
Arbitrage  
\rightarrow  
Price\ Adjustment
}
$$
---

## 23. Arbitrage in Theory and Practice

In theoretical finance, arbitrage is often defined as a riskless profit.

In real markets, practical arbitrage can involve risks such as:

- execution risk;
    
- liquidity risk;
    
- financing risk;
    
- short-selling constraints;
    
- timing differences;
    
- transaction costs.
    

Therefore the pure textbook concept should be distinguished from real-world implementation.

Nevertheless, the no-arbitrage principle remains extraordinarily powerful for pricing financial assets.

The session connected several ideas studied since the beginning of the course.

### Time Value of Money

$$
\text{Value=PV(Future  Cash  Flows)}
$$
### Bond Pricing

Cash flows with different maturities must be discounted consistently.

### Market Efficiency

### Arbitrage

Inconsistent prices create incentives for traders.

### Law of One Price

Identical payoffs imply identical prices.

### Replication

A security can be valued by constructing another portfolio with identical cash flows.

All of these ideas point toward one principle:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Prices  Must  Be  Internally  Consistent}
}
$$

## 25. Five Ideas to Remember

1. **Market efficiency is fundamentally about information.**
    

$$
\text{Price} \approx \text{Information}
$$
2. **Weak, semi-strong and strong efficiency differ according to the information set considered.**
    
3. **When an exploitable inefficiency appears, arbitrage tends to push prices back toward consistency.**
    
4. **The Law of One Price says that identical future cash flows must have identical prices today.**
    

$$
\text{Same Payoffs} \implies \text{Same Price}
$$
5. **Replicating portfolios allow us to price securities using the no-arbitrage principle.**
    

$$
\boxed{
\text{Price of Security} = \text{Value of Replicating Portfolio}
}
$$
