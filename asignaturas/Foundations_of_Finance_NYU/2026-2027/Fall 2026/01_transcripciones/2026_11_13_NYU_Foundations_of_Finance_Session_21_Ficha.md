## 3. Review: From Portfolio Theory to Beta

The previous Portfolio Theory framework measured risk through:

$$
\boxed{
\text{sigma}
}
$$
and portfolio variance:

$$
\boxed{\sigma_p^2 = w_A^2\sigma_A^2 + w_B^2\sigma_B^2 + 2w_A w_B \text{Cov}(A,B)}
$$

This framework is powerful, but mathematically demanding.

The course then moved to a simpler model:

$$
\boxed{
\text{beta}
}
$$
Beta captures how an asset moves relative to the market.

$$
\boxed{
\beta_i =  \frac{\text{Cov}(R_i, R_M)} {\text{Var}(R_M)}
}
$$
The intuition is:

> **The greater the systematic sensitivity of an asset to the market, the greater its beta.**

---

## 4. Different Meanings of Risk

A major conceptual point was that the word **risk** can mean different things depending on the financial instrument.

### Stocks

Risk is often described through:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Volatility}
}
$$
or:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Variance / Standard Deviation}
}
$$
The investor may gain a lot or lose a lot.

### Bonds

Risk can include:

- Credit risk.
    
- Interest-rate risk.
    

For example:

$$
Interest\ Rates\uparrow  
\Rightarrow  
Bond\ Prices\downarrow
$$
The word is the same, but the mechanism is different.

The common element is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Uncertainty}
}
$$
---

## 5. Risk and Return

Regardless of the particular type of risk, the basic financial principle remains:

$$
\boxed{
Higher\ Risk  
\Rightarrow  
Higher\ Required\ Return
}
$$
Portfolio Theory made this relationship mathematically complex.

CAPM simplifies it by focusing on beta:

$$
\boxed{
Higher\ Beta  
\Rightarrow  
Higher\ Required\ Return
}
$$
---

## 6. CAPM Review

CAPM states:

$$
\boxed{E(R_i) = R_f + \beta_i [E(R_M) - R_f]}
$$

where:

- (R_f) = risk-free rate;
    
- (\beta_i) = systematic risk;
    
- (E(R_M)-R_f) = market risk premium.
    

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\text{Required Return} = \text{Risk-Free Rate} + \text{Compensation for Systematic Risk}}
$$

The Security Market Line represents this relationship.

---

## 7. Portfolio Beta

Another simplification introduced by CAPM is that portfolio beta is simply a weighted average:

$$
\boxed{\beta_p = \sum_i w_i \beta_i}
$$

For two assets:

$$
\boxed{\beta_p = w_A \beta_A + w_B \beta_B}
$$

This is much simpler than calculating full portfolio variance using all covariances.

---

## 8. From Total Risk to Systematic Risk

The conceptual move from Portfolio Theory to CAPM was again emphasized.

Total risk contains:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Systematic  Risk  
+  
Idiosyncratic  Risk}
}
$$
Diversification can reduce:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Idiosyncratic Risk}
}
$$
but not:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Systematic Risk}
}
$$
CAPM therefore focuses on systematic risk.

This is the risk investors cannot simply eliminate through diversification.

---

## 9. Efficient Market Hypothesis Review

The class also returned briefly to the Efficient Market Hypothesis.

The essential idea is:

$$
\boxed{\text{Information} \longrightarrow \text{Prices}}
$$

A market is efficient when prices reflect available information.

Three information sets were reviewed:

### Weak Form

Historical prices.

### Semi-Strong Form

All public information.

### Strong Form

Public and private information.

The important lesson remained:

> **The meaning of market efficiency depends on what we define as information.**

---

## 10. Information Is Not Shared Equally

The session emphasized that people do not naturally possess identical information.

Different individuals have:

- different experiences;
    
- different cultures;
    
- different locations;
    
- different knowledge;
    
- different information sources;
    
- different analytical tools.
    

Markets aggregate this dispersed information through prices and trading.

This was used to reinforce the idea that:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Information  
 \rightarrow  
Trading  
 \rightarrow  
Prices}
}
$$
---

## 11. AI as an Information Tool

The class used AI as an example of the changing information environment.

AI can help investors:

- retrieve information;
    
- summarize company reports;
    
- compare data;
    
- identify relationships;
    
- analyze large amounts of material.
    

But an important warning was given:

> **Use AI, but do not let AI do the thinking for you.**

AI outputs may contain biases.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
Tool\ Output  
\neq  
Independent\ Judgment
}
$$
Financial analysis still requires the investor to question the assumptions, sources and framing behind an answer.

## 12. NVIDIA as an Information and Valuation Example

NVIDIA was used as a practical example to discuss information, market efficiency and valuation.

The discussion focused on questions such as:

- customer concentration;
    
- disclosure of major customers;
    
- client capital expenditure;
    
- financing relationships;
    
- sustainability of revenue growth;
    
- sustainability of cash flows;
    
- whether current earnings reflect a temporary investment boom.
    

The point of the discussion was not simply:

> Is NVIDIA cheap or expensive?

The deeper question was:

> **What information are we using when we make that valuation?**

## 13. Revenue Versus Economic Sustainability

The NVIDIA discussion connected directly with an earlier theme of the course:

$$
\boxed{
Accounting\ Numbers  
\neq  
Economic\ Reality
}
$$
High reported revenue or earnings do not automatically answer questions such as:

- Are customers independently financed?
    
- Are capital expenditures sustainable?
    
- Will those customers continue investing?
    
- Will future Free Cash Flow support current valuations?
    

The class emphasized looking beyond one headline number.

---

## 14. Capital Expenditure and Financing

The discussion then moved into CAPEX.

Large technology and infrastructure projects often require:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Large Initial Investment}
}
$$
followed by future revenues and cash flows.

This resembles the capital-budgeting framework studied earlier:

$$
\text{Initial  CAPEX  
 \rightarrow  
Future  Revenues  
 \rightarrow  
Future  Cash  Flows}
$$
The economic question remains:

$$
\boxed{
\text{PV}(\text{Future Cash Flows}) \gt \text{Initial Investment}
}
$$
---

## 15. Beginning Capital Structure

The second major block of the session introduced:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Capital Structure}
}
$$
A company's assets must be financed.

The basic balance-sheet identity is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Assets} = \text{Debt} + \text{Equity}
}
$$
This creates two major providers of capital:

1. Lenders.
    
2. Shareholders.
    

Each requires a return.

---

## 16. Return on Assets

The assets of the company generate an expected return:

$$
\boxed{
R_A
}
$$
This is the economic return generated by the business itself before considering how the assets are financed.

The same operating assets could theoretically be financed through different combinations of:

- debt;
    
- equity.
    

Capital structure affects how the return and risk are distributed between investors.

---

## 17. Debt Is Normally Cheaper Than Equity

Debt generally has lower risk than equity because lenders have a stronger contractual claim.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
R_D \lt R_E
}
$$
in the general framework used in class.

Debt is more secure.

Equity receives the residual return after lenders have been paid.

Therefore shareholders bear more risk and require a higher expected return.

---

## 18. Leverage

If a firm borrows at a relatively low rate and invests in assets producing a higher return, shareholders can obtain a higher return on their equity.

This is financial leverage.

Conceptually:

$$
\boxed{
R_A \gt R_D
}
$$
can allow:

$$
\boxed{
R_E \gt R_A
}
$$
The intuition is that shareholders invest less of their own capital while retaining the residual return after servicing debt.

---

## 19. Leverage Is Not Free

Higher leverage may increase shareholder return, but it also increases shareholder risk.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
Debt\uparrow  
\Rightarrow  
Expected\ Equity\ Return\uparrow
}
$$
but also:

$$
\boxed{
Debt\uparrow  
\Rightarrow  
Equity\ Risk\uparrow
}
$$
Again:

> **More expected return comes with more risk.**

The principle from the beginning of the course remains unchanged.

---

## 20. Asset Beta and Equity Beta

The class distinguished between:

$$
\boxed{
\text{beta_A}
}
$$
the risk of the firm's underlying assets, and:

$$
\boxed{
\text{beta_E}
}
$$
the systematic risk borne by shareholders.

If the firm uses debt, shareholders bear a more leveraged exposure to the underlying business.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\beta_E \gt \beta_A
}
$$
for a leveraged firm under the simplifying assumptions discussed in class.

---

## 21. Levered and Unlevered Beta

The course therefore distinguishes between:

### Unlevered Beta

Risk of the underlying business without financial leverage.

$$
\boxed{
\text{beta_U}
}
$$
### Levered Beta

Risk borne by shareholders after considering debt financing.

$$
\boxed{
\text{beta_L}
}
$$
The central intuition is:

$$
\boxed{
More\ Debt  
\Rightarrow  
Higher\ Levered\ Beta
}
$$
because shareholders absorb more financial risk.

---

## 22. Cost of Equity

Once equity beta is known, CAPM gives the required return demanded by shareholders:

$$
\boxed{R_E = R_f + \beta_E (R_M - R_f)}
$$

Therefore leverage affects the cost of equity indirectly through beta.

If:

$$
\beta_E\uparrow
$$
then:

$$
R_E\uparrow
$$
assuming the other CAPM inputs remain unchanged.

---

## 23. Cost of Debt

Lenders require a return as well.

Conceptually:

$$
\boxed{R_D = R_f + \text{Credit Spread}}
$$

Debt usually carries less risk than equity.

Therefore its required return is normally lower.

But the company must still compensate lenders for:

- time value of money;
    
- credit risk.
    

---

## 24. The Core Capital Structure Trade-Off

Capital structure therefore creates an important relationship.

Debt can be attractive because:

- it is generally cheaper than equity;
    
- it can increase shareholder returns through leverage.
    

But increasing debt also:

- increases financial risk;
    
- increases equity beta;
    
- increases the required return on equity.
    

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Debt  
 \rightarrow  
Cheaper  Financing  
+  
Greater  Equity  Risk}
}
$$
This is one of the central intuitions behind Corporate Finance.

---

## 25. Connection With WACC

The ideas naturally lead back to Weighted Average Cost of Capital:

$$
\boxed{
\text{WACC} = \frac{E}{D + E}R_E + \frac{D}{D + E}R_D(1-T)
}
$$
The firm's assets must generate enough return to compensate both:

- lenders;
    
- shareholders.
    

Therefore WACC represents the weighted required return on the firm's financing.

This connects Capital Structure directly back to valuation.

---

## 26. The Architecture of the Course

Session 21 showed how apparently different topics fit into one structure.

### Step 1 — Finance

$$
\boxed{
\text{Manage an uncertain future}
}
$$
### Step 2 — Risk

Uncertainty generates risk.

### Step 3 — Portfolio Theory

Diversification separates systematic from idiosyncratic risk.

### Step 4 — Beta

Beta measures systematic risk.

### Step 5 — CAPM

Beta determines required equity return.

### Step 6 — Capital Structure

The firm combines debt and equity financing.

### Step 7 — WACC

Debt and equity costs combine into the firm's cost of capital.

### Step 8 — Valuation

That cost of capital becomes the discount rate applied to future cash flows.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Risk  
 \rightarrow  
Required  Return  
 \rightarrow  
Discount  Rate  
 \rightarrow  
Present  Value}
}
$$
---

## 27. Five Ideas to Remember

1. **Risk has different meanings for different securities, but all forms of risk originate in uncertainty.**
    
2. **CAPM simplifies Portfolio Theory by focusing on systematic risk through beta.**
    
3. **Market efficiency depends on information, and financial analysis requires questioning what information is actually available and reliable.**
    
4. **Debt can increase the return earned by shareholders, but it also increases equity risk and equity beta.**
    
5. **Capital structure connects risk, financing and valuation through the cost of debt, cost of equity and WACC.**
    

---

Students should be able to:

- Explain the difference between total risk and systematic risk.
    
- Define beta.
    
- Calculate portfolio beta.

- Explain the three forms of market efficiency.

- Explain why debt is generally cheaper than equity.
    
- Explain financial leverage.
    
- Distinguish asset beta from equity beta.
    
- Explain why leverage increases equity beta.
    
- Connect cost of equity with CAPM.
    
- Connect capital structure with WACC.
    

The key conceptual chain is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Uncertainty  
 \rightarrow  
Risk  
 \rightarrow  
Beta  
 \rightarrow  
Required  Return  
 \rightarrow  
Capital  Structure  
 \rightarrow  
WACC  
 \rightarrow  
Valuation}
}
$$
