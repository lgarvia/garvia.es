# 3. Return

For a one-period investment:

$$\boxed{R = \frac{FV}{PV} - 1}$$

Example:

A stock is bought at:

\$50

and later sold at:

\$75

Then:

$$R = \frac{75}{50} - 1 = 50\%$$

If instead the price falls to:

\$25

then:

$$R = \frac{25}{50} - 1 = -50\%$$

This return formula will connect directly with the Time Value of Money in the next sessions.

---

# 4. Leverage

## What is leverage?

Leverage means:

> **Using borrowed money to finance an investment.**

Suppose an investor wants to buy a \$200 asset.

Instead of using \$200 of personal capital, the investor may finance it with:

$$\$100\text{ equity} + \$100\text{ debt}$$

If the borrowed \$100 costs 5%, the investor must repay:

\$105

after one year.

---

## Why leverage matters

Suppose the \$200 investment grows to \$220.

After repaying the bank:

$\$220 - 105 = 115$$

The investor originally contributed only \$100.

Therefore:

$$R = \frac{115 - 100}{100} = 15\%$$

Without leverage, investing \$200 and receiving \$220 would generate:

10%

Thus:

$$\boxed{\text{Leverage can increase return on equity}}$$

But if the investment performs badly, the debt still has to be repaid.

Therefore:

$$\boxed{\text{Leverage magnifies gains and losses}}$$

---

# 5. Buying on Margin

Buying on margin is a practical form of leverage.

The investor finances a security purchase using:

- personal capital;
    
- money borrowed from the broker.
    

The session used a simplified example in which the investor contributes 50% of the position and borrows the remaining 50%.

Two concepts were introduced:

### Initial Margin

The amount of investor capital required when opening the position.

### Maintenance Margin

The minimum amount of equity that must remain while the position is open.

If equity falls below the required level, the investor may receive a:

## Margin Call

The broker can require:

- additional cash;
    
- additional collateral;
    
- reduction of the position.
    

---

# 6. Long and Short Positions

## Long Position

A long investor benefits when the price rises.

$$\boxed{P\uparrow \;\Rightarrow\; \text{Long investor gains}}$$

The basic strategy is:

1. buy;
    
2. wait;
    
3. sell later.
    

---

## Short Position

A short seller benefits when the price falls.

The simplified process is:

1. borrow shares;
    
2. sell them;
    
3. buy them back later;
    
4. return the shares.
    

Example:

Sell borrowed stock at:

\$50

Buy it back later at:

\$25

Profit:

$\$50 - 25 = 25$$

Therefore:

$$\boxed{P\downarrow \;\Rightarrow\; \text{Short seller gains}}$$

---

# 7. Risk of Short Selling

Short selling can be particularly risky.

For a long investor, the lowest possible stock price is:

\$0

But a stock price can theoretically rise without a fixed upper limit.

Therefore potential losses on a short position can be very large.

The GameStop episode was mentioned as an example of how:

- short positions;
    
- liquidity;
    
- retail investors;
    
- social networks;
    

can interact and produce extreme market movements.

---

# 8. Primary and Secondary Markets

## Primary Market

The primary market is where securities are issued for the first time.

This is where:

$$\boxed{\text{Capital is raised}}$$

For stocks, the key example is:

## IPO — Initial Public Offering

An IPO allows a private company to issue shares and become publicly traded.

---

## Secondary Market

Once securities have been issued, investors trade them among themselves.

Examples include organized stock exchanges.

Therefore:

$$\boxed{\text{Primary Market} = \text{Issuance}} \qquad \boxed{\text{Secondary Market} = \text{Trading}}$$

This distinction is essential.

---

# 9. SPACs

The session briefly introduced:

## SPAC — Special Purpose Acquisition Company

A SPAC raises money first and then looks for a private company to acquire.

Simplified process:

$$\text{SPAC IPO} \rightarrow \text{Search for Company} \rightarrow \text{Acquisition} \rightarrow \text{Company Becomes Public}$$

SPACs became particularly popular during a previous market cycle.

The important lesson is that financial markets can offer different routes for companies to become publicly traded.

---

# 10. OTC Markets

OTC means:

## Over-the-Counter

An OTC transaction takes place outside a centralized public exchange.

The key distinction is:

$$\boxed{\text{OTC} \neq \text{Centralized Exchange}}$$

OTC markets are especially important in areas such as:

- bonds;
    
- currencies;
    
- derivatives.
    

---

# 11. Market Makers

A:

## Market Maker

helps provide liquidity by standing ready to buy and sell securities.

The supermarket analogy used in class is useful:

A supermarket buys products and holds inventory so customers can purchase them when needed.

A market maker performs a similar intermediary role in financial markets.

---

# 12. Bid, Ask and Spread

A market maker normally quotes two prices.

### Bid

The price at which the dealer is willing to **buy**.

### Ask

The price at which the dealer is willing to **sell**.

Normally:

$$\text{Ask} > \text{Bid}$$

The difference is the:

## Bid–Ask Spread

$$\boxed{\text{Spread} = \text{Ask} - \text{Bid}}$$

Example:

$$\text{Bid} = 99 \qquad \text{Ask} = 101$$

Then:

$$\text{Spread} = 2$$

The spread represents part of the cost of immediacy and liquidity.

---

# 13. Order Types

An **order** is an instruction to a broker to buy or sell a security.

Several types were introduced briefly.

## Market Order

Execute immediately at the best available price.

Priority:

$$\boxed{\text{Execution}}$$

---

## Limit Order

Buy or sell only at a specified price or better.

Priority:

$$\boxed{\text{Price}}$$

Execution is not guaranteed.

---

## Stop-Loss Order

An order activated when the security reaches a specified price level.

It can be used as a risk-management tool.

---

## Fill-or-Kill

Execute the entire order immediately or cancel it.

The key lesson is that:

> **How you instruct the broker matters.**

---

# 14. Liquidity

Liquidity is the ability to buy or sell an asset quickly without causing a large price movement.

In a liquid market:

- many buyers and sellers exist;
    
- trading is easier;
    
- spreads are usually smaller.
    

In an illiquid market:

- large trades can move prices substantially;
    
- spreads may be wider.
    

Therefore:

$$\boxed{\text{Liquidity is fundamental to market functioning}}$$

---

# 15. Sovereign Yields and Risk

The session briefly returned to government-bond yields.

The difference between comparable Spanish and German government-bond yields can be interpreted as a measure of relative sovereign risk:

$$\boxed{\text{Spread} = Y_{\text{Spain}} - Y_{\text{Germany}}}$$

The discussion of Japan, the United States and Europe was used mainly to illustrate:

- capital flows;
    
- financing costs;
    
- relative returns;
    
- liquidity.
    

When comparing countries, students must also remember that returns may be denominated in different currencies.

# 16. Core Relationships

## Return

$$\boxed{R = \frac{FV}{PV} - 1}$$

## Leverage

$$\boxed{\text{Debt} + \text{Equity} = \text{Investment}}$$

## Leverage and Risk

$$\boxed{\text{Leverage}\uparrow \;\Rightarrow\; \text{Equity Risk}\uparrow}$$

## Long

$$\boxed{P\uparrow \;\Rightarrow\; \text{Gain}}$$

## Short

$$\boxed{P\downarrow \;\Rightarrow\; \text{Gain}}$$

## Bid–Ask Spread

$$\boxed{\text{Spread} = \text{Ask} - \text{Bid}}$$
