# 5. Financial Markets

## 5.1 What is a market?

A market is a mechanism or place where buyers and sellers can exchange assets.

In financial markets, those assets can include:

- stocks;
    
- bonds;
    
- currencies;
    
- derivatives;
    
- commodities;
    
- cryptocurrencies;
    
- other financial claims.
    

Markets help bring together:

- people who have capital;
    
- people or organizations that need capital.

# 6. OTC Markets

A first piece of financial vocabulary introduced in class was:

## OTC = Over-the-Counter

An **over-the-counter market** is a market in which transactions take place directly between counterparties or through dealer networks rather than through a centralized exchange.

The distinction is useful:

### Exchange-traded market

Transactions occur through an organized exchange.

Examples include exchanges where listed shares or standardized derivatives are traded.

### OTC market

Transactions are arranged outside a centralized public exchange.

OTC markets are particularly important in areas such as:

- bonds;
    
- foreign exchange;
    
- swaps;
    
- other derivatives;
    
- certain cryptocurrency transactions.
    

OTC does **not** mean illegal.

It refers primarily to the **structure through which transactions are conducted**.

---

# 7. Regulation and the SEC

Financial markets are normally subject to regulation.

In the United States, an important institution is the:

## SEC — Securities and Exchange Commission

The SEC plays a major role in:

- supervising securities markets;
    
- enforcing securities law;
    
- protecting investors;
    
- promoting fair and orderly markets;
    
- requiring financial disclosure.
    

A useful distinction for the course is therefore:

> **Market structure and market regulation are related, but they are not the same thing.**

Some markets are highly centralized and heavily supervised.

Others are decentralized.

Regulation also varies significantly between assets and jurisdictions.

Many financial instruments will appear throughout the semester, including:

## 8.1 Stocks

Equity investors normally expect compensation through:

## 8.2 Bonds

A bond is fundamentally a **debt instrument**.

The investor lends money to an issuer, which may be:

- a company;
    
- a government;
    
- another institution.
    

In return, the investor typically receives:

- periodic interest payments, or **coupons**;
    
- repayment of the bond's face value at maturity.
    

Although bonds are usually classified as **fixed income**, their market prices can change significantly before maturity.

One of the most important relationships of the course is:

> **When interest rates rise, bond prices fall.**

And conversely:

> **When interest rates fall, bond prices rise.**

We will later demonstrate this mathematically using present-value formulas.

# 9. Risk and Return

Risk and return are central to finance.

A basic intuition introduced in class was:

> Investments offering greater potential returns normally involve greater uncertainty.

Stocks, for example, may generate higher long-term returns than safer financial assets, but their prices can fluctuate considerably in the short term.

Finance therefore continuously asks:

> **What return am I receiving for the risk I am taking?**

This trade-off between risk and return will become one of the organizing principles of the course.

# 10. Three Basic Investor Principles

The session introduced several fundamental assumptions about investor behavior.

These are simplified assumptions, but they provide a useful starting point for financial theory.

---

## Principle 1 — Investors prefer more wealth to less wealth

If everything else is identical:

> **More money is preferred to less money.**

For example:

- receiving \$110 is better than receiving \$100;
    
- selling an asset for \$120 is better than selling it for \$100.
    

But perspective matters.

A **seller** prefers a higher price.

A **buyer** prefers a lower price.

This simple distinction becomes fundamental in areas such as:

- trading;
    
- investment banking;
    
- mergers and acquisitions;
    
- corporate finance.
    

---

# 11. Mergers and Acquisitions

The acronym introduced in class was:

## M&A = Mergers and Acquisitions

A large part of corporate finance involves transactions in which companies are:

- bought;
    
- sold;
    
- merged;
    
- reorganized.
    

The basic commercial intuition is straightforward:

> **Buy as cheaply as possible and, when selling, sell as expensively as possible.**

Of course, real M&A transactions involve much more sophisticated questions about:

- valuation;
    
- financing;
    
- synergies;
    
- strategy;
    
- risk;
    
- control;
    
- capital structure.
    

But underneath the complexity remains a basic financial question:

> **What is the asset worth relative to the price being paid?**

---

# 12. Principle 2 — Money Today Is Worth More Than Money in the Future

A central concept in finance is:

> **Money has a time value.**

Receiving \$100 today is normally better than receiving \$100 one year from now.

Why?

Because money received today can be invested and generate a return.

This leads directly to one of the most important tools in finance:

# Time Value of Money

We will develop it formally later, but the basic formulas were introduced during this session.

---

# 13. Future Value

Suppose we invest:

$$PV = 100$$

at an annual interest rate:

$$r = 10\%$$

for one year.

After one year:

$$FV = 100(1+0.10)$$

Therefore:

$$FV = 110$$

---

## Two years

If the money remains invested for a second year:

$$FV = 100(1.10)^2$$

Therefore:

$$FV = 121$$

The extra \$1 compared with simply adding \$10 each year comes from **compound interest**.

Interest is earned not only on the original investment but also on previously accumulated interest.

---

# 14. General Future-Value Formula

The general formula is:

$$\boxed{FV = PV(1+r)^t}$$

Where:

- $FV$ = future value;
    
- $PV$ = present value;
    
- $r$ = interest rate per period;
    
- $t$ = number of periods.
    

This formula will appear repeatedly throughout the course.

---

# 15. Present Value

We can reverse the previous problem.

Suppose we will receive:

$$FV = 121$$

two years from now.

If the interest rate is 10%, what is that future payment worth **today**?

We calculate:

$$PV = \frac{121}{(1.10)^2}$$

Therefore:

$$PV = 100$$

---

# 16. General Present-Value Formula

$$\boxed{PV = \frac{FV}{(1+r)^t}}$$

This is one of the fundamental formulas in finance.

The process of calculating present value is called:

## Discounting

A very large part of finance consists of:

> **Taking future cash flows and converting them into values today.**

---

# 17. Why Present Value Is So Important

Present-value calculations will appear in many parts of the course.

### Bond valuation

A bond produces future:

- coupons;
    
- principal repayment.
    

We value the bond by calculating the present value of those future payments.

### Equity valuation

Companies generate future cash flows.

Valuation attempts to determine what those future cash flows are worth today.

### Corporate investment decisions

Companies invest today because they expect future benefits.

Those future benefits must be translated into present values before they can be compared with the investment required today.

This leads to a powerful unifying idea:

> **Finance is largely about moving money through time in a logically consistent way.**

---

# 18. Interest Rates and Present Value

The present-value formula also gives us an important relationship:

$$PV = \frac{FV}{(1+r)^t}$$

If $r$ increases while everything else remains unchanged, the denominator becomes larger.

Therefore:

$$\boxed{r\uparrow \;\Rightarrow\; PV\downarrow}$$

And:

$$\boxed{r\downarrow \;\Rightarrow\; PV\uparrow}$$

This explains one of the most important relationships in fixed income:

$$\boxed{\text{Interest rates }\uparrow \;\Rightarrow\; \text{Bond prices }\downarrow}$$

This relationship will be repeated many times during the semester.

---

# 19. Principle 3 — Investors Are Risk Averse

Another fundamental assumption introduced was:

> **When two investments offer the same expected payoff, investors prefer the less risky investment.**

Consider two alternatives.

### Investment A

Receive:

\$100

with certainty.

### Investment B

- 50% probability of receiving \$200;
    
- 50% probability of receiving \$0.
    

Expected payoff:

$$E(B) = 0.5(200) + 0.5(0) = 100$$

Both alternatives therefore have the same expected payoff:

$$E(A) = E(B) = 100$$

But Investment A has no uncertainty.

Investment B is risky.

A risk-averse investor therefore prefers:

$$\boxed{\$100\text{ for certain}}$$

---

# 20. Risk Aversion Does Not Mean Avoiding All Risk

This distinction is important.

Being risk averse does **not** mean that investors will never accept risk.

Suppose the risky investment instead offered:

- 50% probability of \$201;
    
- 50% probability of \$0.
    

Its expected value would now be:

$\$0.5(201) = 100.5$$

The expected payoff is now greater.

Whether an investor prefers it depends on:

- the additional expected return;
    
- the additional risk;
    
- the investor's personal risk preferences.
    

This idea eventually leads to:

- utility;
    
- risk premiums;
    
- portfolio selection;
    
- risk-return optimization.
    

---

# 21. Price Versus Value

Another distinction introduced near the end of the session was:

> **Price and value are not the same thing.**

## Price

Price is what an asset can currently be bought or sold for in the market.

It is observable.

For a sufficiently liquid asset, we normally speak about a market price.

---

## Value

Value is an assessment of what something is worth.

Value depends on:

- expectations;
    
- information;
    
- preferences;
    
- risk;
    
- circumstances;
    
- assumptions.
    

Therefore:

> There may be **one market price but many estimates of value**.

Different investors may value the same company differently.

---

## Example: a bottle of water

A bottle of water may have a market price of €2.

But its subjective value depends on circumstances.

For someone sitting beside a fountain, its value may be low.

For someone extremely thirsty in the middle of a desert, its value could be enormous.

Therefore:

$$\boxed{\text{Price} \neq \text{Value}}$$

This distinction will become fundamental when studying valuation.

---

# 22. Arbitrage

One of the most important concepts introduced during the session was:

## Arbitrage

Arbitrage involves exploiting inconsistent prices in order to generate a payoff without taking the corresponding economic risk.

In an idealized setting, arbitrage can be thought of as:

> **A strategy requiring zero net investment that produces a non-negative payoff in every scenario and a positive payoff in at least one scenario.**

In introductory examples we can simplify this intuition to:

> **Getting the same thing more cheaply by buying its components or buying the package and selling the components separately.**

---

# 23. The Happy Meal Example

Suppose a hypothetical meal contains:

|Component|Stand-alone price|
|---|--:|
|Burger|\$5|
|Fries|\$3|
|Drink|\$2|
|Toy|\$1|
|**Total**|**\$11**|

If the complete meal were sold for:

\$8

while its components could immediately be sold separately for:

\$11

an arbitrage opportunity would exist.

An arbitrageur could:

1. buy the meal for \$8;
    
2. separate the components;
    
3. sell them for \$11;
    
4. earn \$3.
    

As investors exploit this opportunity:

- demand for the underpriced package increases;
    
- its price tends to rise;
    
- supply of the components increases;
    
- component prices may fall.
    

The price inconsistency therefore tends to disappear.

---

# 24. Arbitrage and Market Efficiency

This example leads to an important idea:

> **In an efficient market, obvious arbitrage opportunities should not persist.**

Why?

Because investors have incentives to exploit them.

Their trades push prices back toward consistency.

This will later connect to the concept of:

## Market Efficiency

For now, retain the intuition:

$$\boxed{\text{Arbitrage opportunities} \rightarrow \text{trading} \rightarrow \text{price adjustment}}$$

---

# 25. Parasol and Umbrella Example

A second exercise demonstrated arbitrage using contingent future payoffs.

Suppose there are two assets:

- a **parasol**;
    
- an **umbrella**.
    

Each costs:

\$100

today.

Their future values depend on the weather.

A simplified version of the example is:

|Asset|Rain|Sun|
|---|--:|--:|
|Parasol|\$90|\$120|
|Umbrella|\$120|\$90|

---

## Portfolio

Buy:

- one parasol;
    
- one umbrella.
    

Initial cost:

$\$100 + 100 = 200$$

Future value if it rains:

$\$90 + 120 = 210$$

Future value if it is sunny:

$\$120 + 90 = 210$$

Therefore the combined portfolio produces:

\$210

regardless of the weather.

The risky individual assets have therefore been combined into a portfolio with a certain future payoff.

---

# 26. Introducing Borrowing

Suppose we can borrow money at an interest rate of:

4%

Borrow:

\$200

today.

Amount to repay one period later:

$\$200(1.04) = 208$$

Use the borrowed \$200 to purchase:

- one parasol;
    
- one umbrella.
    

The portfolio pays:

\$210

in either scenario.

The loan repayment is:

\$208

Therefore:

$\$210 - 208 = 2$$

The strategy produces:

$$\boxed{\$2}$$

regardless of whether it rains or is sunny.

This illustrates how pricing inconsistencies can create an arbitrage opportunity.

---

# 27. Leverage

The previous exercise also introduced another essential concept:

## Leverage

Leverage means using borrowed money to finance an investment.

In the example:

- the investor does not provide the \$200 personally;
    
- the investor borrows the \$200;
    
- the borrowed money finances the portfolio.
    

Leverage can amplify investment opportunities.

But in real financial markets it can also amplify losses.

We will return to leverage repeatedly throughout the semester.

---

# 28. Cash-Flow Sign Convention

An important practical point appeared in the Excel exercise.

When analyzing financial transactions:

### Cash received

Represent it as positive:

++

### Cash paid

Represent it as negative:

−-

For example:

Receiving a \$200 loan today:

+200

Repaying \$208 later:

-208

Thinking systematically in terms of **cash inflows and cash outflows** will simplify many future exercises.

# 29. Market Efficiency and the Law of One Price

The discussion of arbitrage leads naturally to the:

## Law of One Price

If two assets generate exactly the same future cash flows under the same conditions, they should have the same price today.

Otherwise an arbitrage opportunity would exist.

In simplified notation:

If:

$$CF_A = CF_B$$

then in an efficient market:

$$P_A = P_B$$

This principle will become fundamental in valuation.

# 35. Automation, AI, and Thinking

A particularly important distinction was made regarding AI.

Before modern AI tools, people often:

1. started performing a task;
    
2. spent considerable time doing it;
    
3. thought about the problem while working.
    

AI can now perform many tasks extremely quickly.

This changes where human effort should be concentrated.

Instead of:

> **Do first → Think while doing**

the better model increasingly becomes:

> **Think deeply first → Then execute quickly**

AI therefore does **not** eliminate the need to think.

In many situations it makes thinking **more important**.

# 37. Why Students Must Solve Exercises Themselves

Financial reasoning develops through repeated practice.

A student may ask an AI system to solve an exercise instantly.

But if the objective is to learn finance, this can be counterproductive.

The relevant question is not:

> “Can AI solve this problem?”

It probably can.

The relevant question is:

> **“Can I solve and understand this problem myself?”**

AI can then be used effectively to:

- explain concepts;
    
- check reasoning;
    
- identify mistakes;
    
- generate additional exercises;
    
- explore alternative approaches;
    
- review material.
    

But it should not eliminate the learning process.

# 56. Key Mathematical Relationships Introduced

Students do not need complete mastery after Session 1, but should recognize the following formulas.

## Future Value

$$\boxed{FV = PV(1+r)^t}$$

---

## Present Value

$$\boxed{PV = \frac{FV}{(1+r)^t}}$$

---

## Interest rates and present value

$$\boxed{r\uparrow \;\Rightarrow\; PV\downarrow}$$

---

## Bond prices and interest rates

$$\boxed{r\uparrow \;\Rightarrow\; P_{\text{bond}}\downarrow}$$

and:

$$\boxed{r\downarrow \;\Rightarrow\; P_{\text{bond}}\uparrow}$$

---

## Expected payoff

For possible outcomes $X_i$ with probabilities $p_i$:

$$\boxed{E(X) = \sum_i p_i X_i}$$

Example:

$$E(X) = 0.5(200) + 0.5(0) = 100$$

# 63. Final Takeaway

The purpose of the first session was not to master finance in ninety minutes.

It was to begin learning how finance **thinks**.

Financial decisions involve:

$$\boxed{\text{Money}}$$

moving through:

$$\boxed{\text{Time}}$$

under:

$$\boxed{\text{Uncertainty}}$$

Finance provides the tools to decide:

- what future cash flows are worth today;
    
- how much risk is acceptable;
    
- whether an asset is cheap or expensive;
    
- whether two prices are mutually consistent;
    
- whether borrowing creates or destroys value;
    
- how investors and corporations should allocate capital.
    

During the semester, the apparently separate ideas introduced today will increasingly connect with each other.

The objective is to reach the point where concepts such as:

**present value, risk, return, stocks, bonds, leverage, arbitrage, liquidity, market efficiency, and valuation**

are no longer isolated vocabulary.

They become part of a single financial framework for making decisions.
