## 2. The Transition: From Valuation to Risk

The first half of the course was anchored by the classical discounted cash flow (DCF) identity:

$$
\boxed{PV = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t}}
$$

In all previous applications—pricing fixed-coupon bonds, discounting growing dividends via the Gordon model, or computing the Net Present Value (NPV) of corporate capital budgeting projects—the discount rate $r$ was taken as a known parameter.

Now, we address the foundational question:

$$
\boxed{\text{What determines the discount rate } r\text{?}}
$$

In financial economics, the discount rate represents the **opportunity cost of capital**—the required rate of return that investors demand as compensation for bearing risk. To explain $r$, the second half of the course follows this progression:

$$
\boxed{\text{Risk \& Volatility} \longrightarrow \text{Portfolio Theory} \longrightarrow \text{CAPM \& Beta} \longrightarrow \text{Required Return } (r) \longrightarrow \text{Asset Valuation}}
$$

The valuation machinery remains unchanged. What changes is that we now explain the pricing of risk itself.

## 3. Midterm Review: Holding Period Return (HPR)

The session opened with a review of representative questions from the midterm examination.

### 3.1 General Definition

The Holding Period Return measures the total percentage change in wealth generated by holding an asset over a given time horizon:

$$
\boxed{HPR = \frac{\text{Ending Wealth} - \text{Beginning Wealth}}{\text{Beginning Wealth}} = \frac{\text{Ending Wealth}}{\text{Beginning Wealth}} - 1}
$$

Ending wealth must account for **all cash received while holding the asset**, not merely its market capital gain or loss:
- **For equities**:
  $$
  \text{Ending Wealth} = P_{\text{end}} + \text{Dividends}
  $$
  $$
  HPR = \frac{P_1 - P_0 + D_1}{P_0} = \underbrace{\frac{P_1 - P_0}{P_0}}_{\text{Capital Gains Yield}} + \underbrace{\frac{D_1}{P_0}}_{\text{Dividend Yield}}
  $$
- **For bonds**:
  $$
  \text{Ending Wealth} = P_{\text{sale}} + \text{Coupons} + \text{Reinvestment Income}
  $$

## 4. Midterm Review: Bond HPR with Reinvestment (Exercise 18)

A key exam exercise evaluated the annualized HPR of an investor who purchases a bond, receives coupons, reinvests the proceeds, and sells prior to maturity.

### Problem Parameters:
- **Maturity**: 3-year bond.
- **Face Value ($FV$)**: $\$1{,}000$.
- **Annual Coupon Rate**: $5\%$ ($\text{Coupon} = 0.05 \times 1{,}000 = \$50$ per year).
- **Initial Yield to Maturity ($\text{YTM}_0$)**: $5\%$.
- **Coupon 1 Reinvestment**: Reinvested for 1 year at an annual rate of $2\%$.
- **Holding Horizon**: Sold after 2 years ($t = 2$).
- **Sale Yield ($\text{YTM}_2$)**: At $t = 2$, prevailing market yields fall to $3\%$.

### Step-by-Step Resolution:

1. **Initial Purchase Price ($P_0$)**:  
   Because the initial coupon rate equals the initial $\text{YTM}$ ($5\% = 5\%$), the bond trades at par:
   $$
   P_0 = \$1{,}000
   $$

2. **Reinvestment of the First Coupon ($t = 1 \to t = 2$)**:  
   The $\$50$ coupon received at the end of Year 1 is reinvested for one year at $2\%$:
   $$
   FV(C_1)_{t=2} = 50 \times (1 + 0.02) = \$51.00
   $$

3. **Second Coupon Received ($t = 2$)**:  
   At Year 2, the investor receives the second $\$50$ coupon in cash:
   $$
   C_2 = \$50.00
   $$

4. **Sale Price of the Bond ($P_2$)**:  
   At $t = 2$, exactly 1 year remains until maturity. The remaining cash flow is the par value plus the final coupon: $\$1{,}000 + \$50 = \$1{,}050$. Discounted at the new prevailing yield of $3\%$:
   $$
   P_2 = \frac{1{,}050}{1 + 0.03} = \frac{1{,}050}{1.03} \approx \$1{,}019.417
   $$

5. **Total Accumulated Wealth at $t = 2$**:
   $$
   FV_{\text{total}} = FV(C_1) + C_2 + P_2 = 51.00 + 50.00 + 1{,}019.417 = \$1{,}120.417
   $$

6. **Annualized Holding Period Return ($T = 2$ years)**:
   $$
   \boxed{HPR_{\text{annualized}} = \left( \frac{FV_{\text{total}}}{P_0} \right)^{1/2} - 1 = \left( \frac{1{,}120.417}{1{,}000} \right)^{1/2} - 1 = \sqrt{1.120417} - 1 \approx 5.85\%}
   $$

> **Key Takeaway**: Bond returns over multi-year holding periods depend on three distinct components:
> 1. Coupon income.
> 2. Reinvestment rate earned on intermediate coupons.
> 3. Capital gain or loss upon sale, dictated by the yield shift (yield dropped from $5\%$ to $3\%$, producing a capital gain from $\$1{,}000$ to $\$1{,}019.42$).

## 5. Midterm Review: Perpetuity HPR & The Income Component (Exercise 3)

Another classic exam problem demonstrated how easily investors overlook intermediate cash flows.

### Problem Parameters:
- A perpetuity pays $C = \$50$ annually forever, starting one year from purchase ($t = 1$).
- Purchased when the market discount rate is $r_0 = 5\%$.
- Exactly one year later ($t = 1$), immediately after collecting the first $\$50$ cash payment, the market discount rate falls to $r_1 = 4\%$, and the asset is sold.

### Step-by-Step Resolution:

1. **Purchase Price ($P_0$)**:
   $$
   P_0 = \frac{C}{r_0} = \frac{50}{0.05} = \$1{,}000
   $$

2. **Sale Price One Year Later ($P_1$)**:  
   Since the perpetuity still pays $\$50$ per year forever, at the new discount rate of $4\%$:
   $$
   P_1 = \frac{C}{r_1} = \frac{50}{0.04} = \$1{,}250
   $$

3. **Total Holding Period Return**:  
   Between $t = 0$ and $t = 1$, the investor received both the sale proceeds $P_1$ and the cash payment $C_1$:
   $$
   \boxed{HPR = \frac{P_1 + C_1}{P_0} - 1 = \frac{1{,}250 + 50}{1{,}000} - 1 = \frac{1{,}300}{1{,}000} - 1 = 30\%}
   $$

> **Common Student Mistake**: Calculating $\frac{1{,}250}{1{,}000} - 1 = 25\%$ by looking only at the capital gain ($P_1 - P_0$) while forgetting the $\$50$ cash income already in pocket! The total return comprises a $25\%$ capital gain plus a $5\%$ income yield $= 30\%$.

## 6. Midterm Review: Gordon Growth Model & Expected Return (Exercise 13)

### Problem Parameters:
- Current stock price: $P_0 = \$40$.
- Dividend just paid: $D_0 = \$2.00$.
- Constant perpetual growth rate: $g = 3\%$.

### Step-by-Step Resolution:

1. **Compute Expected Dividend in Year 1 ($D_1$)**:
   $$
   D_1 = D_0 (1 + g) = 2.00 \times (1 + 0.03) = \$2.06
   $$

2. **Isolate Required Return $r$**:  
   From the Gordon Growth Model:
   $$
   P_0 = \frac{D_1}{r - g} \implies r = \frac{D_1}{P_0} + g
   $$

3. **Compute $r$**:
   $$
   \boxed{r = \frac{2.06}{40} + 0.03 = 0.0515 + 0.03 = 8.15\%}
   $$

> **Common Student Mistake**: Inserting $D_0 = \$2.00$ directly into the numerator rather than growing it to $D_1 = \$2.06$, which yields an incorrect return of $8.00\%$.

## 7. Arithmetic Versus Geometric Returns

When evaluating returns over multiple periods, the method of averaging is crucial.

### The Problem:
Consider an asset with the following annual returns over a 2-year horizon:
- **Year 1**: $+100\%$
- **Year 2**: $-50\%$

### 1. Arithmetic Average Return:
$$
\bar{r}_A = \frac{r_1 + r_2}{2} = \frac{1.00 + (-0.50)}{2} = \frac{0.50}{2} = +25\%
$$

### 2. Actual Wealth Evolution:
Suppose an investor starts with $\$1{,}000$:
- End of Year 1 ($+100\%$):
  $$
  W_1 = 1{,}000 \times (1 + 1.00) = \$2{,}000
  $$
- End of Year 2 ($-50\%$):
  $$
  W_2 = 2{,}000 \times (1 - 0.50) = \$1{,}000
  $$

After two years, the investor has exactly $\$1{,}000$—zero net wealth gained or lost.

### 3. Geometric Average Return (Compound Annual Growth Rate):
$$
\bar{r}_G = \left[ \prod_{t=1}^{T} (1 + r_t) \right]^{1/T} - 1 = \left[ (1 + 1.00)(1 - 0.50) \right]^{1/2} - 1 = \sqrt{2 \times 0.50} - 1 = \sqrt{1} - 1 = 0\%
$$

$$
\boxed{\bar{r}_G = 0\%}
$$

### Comparison & Economic Implication:
| Metric | Value | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Arithmetic Average ($\bar{r}_A$)** | $+25\%$ | Best estimate of the expected return in any single isolated upcoming period. |
| **Geometric Average ($\bar{r}_G$)** | $0\%$ | True compound per-period rate of wealth accumulation over multi-period horizons. |

> **Key Rule**: $\bar{r}_A \ge \bar{r}_G$ always holds, with equality occurring only when returns across all periods are identical. The difference $(\bar{r}_A - \bar{r}_G) \approx \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2$ is driven directly by volatility. For performance reporting and compounding wealth, **geometric returns** are the accurate measure.

## 8. What Is Risk? Uncertainty vs. Volatility

In everyday conversation, "risk" refers to general danger or downside. In finance, we define risk with mathematical precision:

1. **Broad Concept: Uncertainty**:
   $$
   \boxed{\text{Risk} = \text{Uncertainty about future outcomes}}
   $$
   We do not know which future state of the world will materialize. For bonds, risk arises from interest rate and inflation shifts; for equities, from macroeconomic cycles and firm cash flows.

2. **Modern Portfolio Theory: Volatility**:
   $$
   \boxed{\text{Risk} \equiv \text{Volatility of Returns} = \sigma}
   $$
   Under Markowitz Portfolio Theory, risk is operationalized as the **statistical dispersion of possible returns around their expected value**. The wider the spread of possible outcomes, the greater the risk.

### The Fundamental Risk-Return Trade-Off:
$$
\boxed{\text{Higher Expected Return} \iff \text{Bearing Higher Volatility / Risk}}
$$

Investors are assumed to be **risk-averse**: they dislike volatility. Consequently, capital markets must offer a higher expected return (risk premium) to induce investors to hold volatile assets.

## 9. Scenario Analysis & Expected Return

To quantify risk and return, we model the future through discrete economic scenarios (states of the world).

Let:
- $n$ = number of possible economic scenarios.
- $p_i$ = probability of scenario $i$, where $\sum_{i=1}^n p_i = 1$ and $p_i \ge 0$.
- $R_i$ = realized return of the asset if scenario $i$ occurs.

### Expected Return ($E(R)$):
The expected return is the probability-weighted average of the scenario returns:

$$
\boxed{E(R) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i R_i}
$$

> **Crucial Distinction**: Expected return is a mathematical expectation, not a guaranteed return. The investor may never realize exactly $E(R)$ in any individual state; $E(R)$ represents the central tendency of the probability distribution.

---

## 10. Measuring Dispersion: Variance and Standard Deviation

To quantify how far possible outcomes can deviate from $E(R)$, we calculate variance and standard deviation.

### Why Not Simple Average Deviations?
If we simply averaged deviations $(R_i - E(R))$:
$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i [R_i - E(R)] = \sum p_i R_i - E(R) \sum p_i = E(R) - E(R) = 0
$$
Positive deviations and negative deviations cancel each other out identically.

### 1. Variance ($\sigma^2$ or $\text{Var}(R)$):
To eliminate cancellation, deviations are squared before weighting by probabilities:

$$
\boxed{\sigma^2 = \text{Var}(R) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i [R_i - E(R)]^2}
$$

### 2. Standard Deviation ($\sigma$):
Because variance is denominated in squared units ($\%^2$), we take the positive square root to restore the original units of measurement (percentage return):

$$
\boxed{\sigma = \sqrt{\text{Var}(R)} = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i [R_i - E(R)]^2}}
$$

In Portfolio Theory:
$$
\boxed{\text{Higher } \sigma \implies \text{Greater Return Dispersion} \implies \text{Higher Risk}}
$$

## 11. Complete Case Study: Walmart (WMT) vs. Target (TGT)

To illustrate the mechanics, the class examined a macroeconomic model with three discrete states of nature.

### Macroeconomic Probabilities:
| Scenario ($i$) | State of the Economy | Probability ($p_i$) |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| 1 | Recession | $10\%$ ($0.10$) |
| 2 | Normal Growth | $50\%$ ($0.50$) |
| 3 | Strong Boom | $40\%$ ($0.40$) |
| **Total** | | **$100\%$ ($1.00$)** |

### 11.1 Walmart Analysis

Walmart represents a defensive consumer staple retailer with resilient demand during downturns:
- **Recession ($p_1 = 0.10$)**: $R_{W,1} = 0\%$
- **Normal ($p_2 = 0.50$)**: $R_{W,2} = 10\%$
- **Boom ($p_3 = 0.40$)**: $R_{W,3} = 20\%$

#### 1. Expected Return for Walmart:
$$
E(R_W) = (0.10 \times 0.00) + (0.50 \times 0.10) + (0.40 \times 0.20) = 0.00 + 0.05 + 0.08 = 0.13 = 13\%
$$

#### 2. Variance and Standard Deviation Table for Walmart:
| State ($i$) | $p_i$ | $R_{W,i}$ | Deviation: $[R_{W,i} - E(R_W)]$ | Squared Deviation: $[R_{W,i} - E(R_W)]^2$ | Weighted Squared Dev: $p_i [R_i - E(R)]^2$ |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Recession | $0.10$ | $0.00$ | $0.00 - 0.13 = -0.13$ | $(-0.13)^2 = 0.0169$ | $0.10 \times 0.0169 = 0.00169$ |
| Normal | $0.50$ | $0.10$ | $0.10 - 0.13 = -0.03$ | $(-0.03)^2 = 0.0009$ | $0.50 \times 0.0009 = 0.00045$ |
| Boom | $0.40$ | $0.20$ | $0.20 - 0.13 = +0.07$ | $(+0.07)^2 = 0.0049$ | $0.40 \times 0.0049 = 0.00196$ |
| **Sum** | **$1.00$** | | | **Variance ($\sigma_W^2$)** | **$0.00410$** |

$$
\sigma_W^2 = 0.00410
$$

$$
\boxed{\sigma_W = \sqrt{0.00410} \approx 0.06403 = 6.403\%}
$$

## 12. Normal Distribution Intuition & The Empirical Rule

Although returns in reality can exhibit skewness and fat tails (kurtosis), the normal distribution provides invaluable intuition for interpreting $\sigma$:

- **$1\sigma$ Band**: Approximately **$68.3\%$** (roughly two-thirds) of realizations fall within $E(R) \pm 1\sigma$.
- **$2\sigma$ Band**: Approximately **$95.4\%$** of realizations fall within $E(R) \pm 2\sigma$.
- **$3\sigma$ Band**: Approximately **$99.7\%$** of realizations fall within $E(R) \pm 3\sigma$.

### Application to Walmart ($E(R_W) = 13\%$, $\sigma_W \approx 6.40\%$):
- $1\sigma$ Interval: $[13\% - 6.40\%, 13\% + 6.40\%] = \mathbf{[6.60\%, 19.40\%]}$
- $2\sigma$ Interval: $[13\% - 2(6.40\%), 13\% + 2(6.40\%)] = \mathbf{[0.19\%, 25.81\%]}$
- $3\sigma$ Interval: $[13\% - 3(6.40\%), 13\% + 3(6.40\%)] = \mathbf{[-6.21\%, 32.21\%]}$

This demonstrates why standard deviation is the universal language of risk: it establishes quantifiable confidence bands around our expectations.

## 13. Covariance: Measuring Joint Movement

Variance measures the risk of a single isolated asset. However, investors hold **portfolios** of assets.

To determine portfolio risk, we must measure how assets **co-vary** (move together):

$$
\boxed{\text{Cov}(A,B) = \sigma_{AB} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i [R_{A,i} - E(R_A)][R_{B,i} - E(R_B)]}
$$

### Interpretation of Covariance:
- **$\text{Cov}(A,B) > 0$**: Assets move in the same direction. When asset $A$ exceeds its mean, asset $B$ also tends to exceed its mean.
- **$\text{Cov}(A,B) < 0$**: Assets move in opposite directions. When asset $A$ rises above its mean, asset $B$ tends to fall below its mean (powerful hedge).
- **$\text{Cov}(A,B) = 0$**: No linear relationship between the deviations of the two assets.

### Calculation for Walmart and Target:
| State ($i$) | $p_i$ | $[R_{W,i} - E(R_W)]$ | $[R_{T,i} - E(R_T)]$ | Product of Deviations | Weighted Product: $p_i \Delta_W \Delta_T$ |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Recession | $0.10$ | $-0.13$ | $-0.26$ | $(-0.13) \times (-0.26) = +0.0338$ | $0.10 \times 0.0338 = 0.00338$ |
| Normal | $0.50$ | $-0.03$ | $-0.06$ | $(-0.03) \times (-0.06) = +0.0018$ | $0.50 \times 0.0018 = 0.00090$ |
| Boom | $0.40$ | $+0.07$ | $+0.14$ | $(+0.07) \times (+0.14) = +0.0098$ | $0.40 \times 0.0098 = 0.00392$ |
| **Covariance** | | | | | **$\text{Cov}(W,T) = \mathbf{0.00820}$** |

$$
\boxed{\text{Cov}(W,T) = 0.00820}
$$

---

## 14. Correlation: The Standardized Relationship

Covariance has a major practical limitation: its numerical magnitude depends on the units and volatility of the individual assets, making it unstandardized and difficult to interpret directly.

To overcome this, we define the **correlation coefficient** ($\rho_{A,B}$):

$$
\boxed{\rho_{A,B} = \frac{\text{Cov}(A,B)}{\sigma_A \sigma_B}}
$$

### Mathematical Bounds:
$$
\boxed{-1 \le \rho_{A,B} \le +1}
$$

- **$\rho = +1.0$ (Perfect Positive Correlation)**: The assets move in identical linear lockstep. No risk reduction from diversification beyond simple weighted averaging.
- **$\rho = 0.0$ (Uncorrelated)**: No linear comovement. Substantial diversification benefits.
- **$\rho = -1.0$ (Perfect Negative Correlation)**: Assets move in perfect mirror opposition. It is theoretically possible to construct a portfolio with **zero volatility**.

### Computation for Walmart and Target:
$$
\rho_{W,T} = \frac{\text{Cov}(W,T)}{\sigma_W \sigma_T} = \frac{0.00820}{0.06403 \times 0.12806} = \frac{0.00820}{0.00820} = +1.00
$$

$$
\boxed{\rho_{W,T} = +1.00}
$$

> **Note from Lecture**: In this stylized classroom example, Target's deviations from its mean are exactly twice Walmart's deviations in every single state:
> - Recession: $-0.26 = 2 \times (-0.13)$
> - Normal: $-0.06 = 2 \times (-0.03)$
> - Boom: $+0.14 = 2 \times (+0.07)$
>
> Because the linear scaling is exact ($R_T - E(R_T) = 2[R_W - E(R_W)]$), the correlation coefficient is mathematically $+1.00$. In real equity markets, retail competitors share common macroeconomic drivers and exhibit strong positive correlation (typically $\rho \approx 0.70 - 0.85$), but not exact $+1.00$.

---

## 15. Why Correlation Matters: The Key to Diversification

This introduces the core revelation of Modern Portfolio Theory:

> **The risk of a portfolio depends far more on how the assets move together (covariance / correlation) than on the individual variances of the assets themselves.**

When assets have correlation $\rho < +1$:
1. Some assets experience positive deviations while others experience negative deviations.
2. Independent fluctuations partially cancel each other out.
3. Total portfolio risk ($\sigma_p$) falls **below** the weighted average volatility of the individual components, while expected return remains a linear weighted average.

This is the famous economic principle: **diversification is the only "free lunch" in finance**.

## 17. The Statistical Vocabulary of Modern Finance

The second half of the course relies on this core mathematical toolkit:

| Concept | Symbol | Mathematical Formula | Economic Interpretation |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| **Expected Return** | $E(R)$ | $\sum p_i R_i$ | Center of gravity; expected payoff. |
| **Variance** | $\text{Var}(R)$ or $\sigma^2$ | $\sum p_i [R_i - E(R)]^2$ | Average squared dispersion of outcomes. |
| **Standard Deviation** | $\sigma$ | $\sqrt{\text{Var}(R)}$ | **Volatility** in percentage units; stand-alone risk. |
| **Covariance** | $\text{Cov}(A,B)$ or $\sigma_{AB}$ | $\sum p_i [R_{A,i} - E(R_A)][R_{B,i} - E(R_B)]$ | Measure of directional comovement. |
| **Correlation** | $\rho_{A,B}$ | $\frac{\text{Cov}(A,B)}{\sigma_A \sigma_B}$ | Standardized comovement bounded in $[-1, +1]$. |

---

## 18. Five Key Takeaways to Remember

1. **The Origin of the Discount Rate**: The second half of the course shifts from "given $r$, what is $PV$?" to "how does the market determine $r$ based on risk?".
2. **Income Matters for HPR**: Holding Period Return must always include intermediate cash flows received (coupons, dividends) and their reinvestment.
3. **Geometric vs. Arithmetic Return**: Arithmetic averages describe single-period expectations; geometric averages describe multi-period compounding and true wealth growth.
4. **Risk Is Volatility**: In Modern Portfolio Theory, risk is defined as the dispersion of returns around their mean ($\sigma$).
5. **Portfolio Risk Depends on Joint Movement**: The risk of a collection of assets is governed primarily by covariance and correlation, making diversification the engine of portfolio efficiency.
