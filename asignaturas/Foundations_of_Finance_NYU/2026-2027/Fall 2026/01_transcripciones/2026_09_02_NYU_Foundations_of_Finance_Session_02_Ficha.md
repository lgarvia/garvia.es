Session 2 continued the introduction to finance and moved from the broad principles introduced in Session 1 toward the actual **financial instruments and markets** that will be studied throughout the semester.

The most important technical message of the session was:

> **The price of a financial asset is determined by the present value of the future cash flows that the asset promises to its owner.**

$$\boxed{P_{\text{bond}} = PV(\text{coupons}) + PV(\text{principal})}$$

This idea will be repeated and developed extensively later in the course.

# 2. Connection with Session 1

Session 2 began by reviewing the three basic assumptions about investor behavior introduced during Session 1.

These assumptions provide a simplified framework for thinking about financial decisions.

---

# 3. Investor Axiom 1 — More Wealth Is Preferred to Less Wealth

Under otherwise identical circumstances:

> **Investors prefer more money to less money.**

If two investments have the same:

- risk;
    
- timing;
    
- characteristics;
    

but one pays more, the investor will prefer the investment that pays more.

This sounds obvious, but many financial models begin from similarly simple assumptions.

---

# 4. Investor Axiom 2 — Less Risk Is Preferred to More Risk

If two investments offer the same expected payoff:

> **A risk-averse investor prefers the less risky investment.**

This does not mean investors refuse to take risk.

It means that investors normally require **additional expected return** as compensation for accepting additional risk.

This establishes one of the most important relationships in finance:

$$\boxed{\text{More Risk} \Rightarrow \text{More Required Return}}$$

---

# 5. Investor Axiom 3 — Money Today Is Preferred to Money in the Future

Under otherwise identical conditions:

> **Money today is worth more than the same amount of money received later.**

Why?

Because money available today can:

- be invested;
    
- generate interest;
    
- be consumed;
    
- provide liquidity;
    
- eliminate uncertainty.
    

This is the foundation of the:

## Time Value of Money

and explains why future payments must be **discounted**.

---

# 6. Review: Price Versus Value

Session 2 also returned to the distinction introduced at the end of Session 1.

## Price

The **price** of an asset is the amount at which it can currently be exchanged in the market.

Price is:

- observable;
    
- market-based;
    
- normally common to buyers and sellers at a particular moment.
    

---

## Value

The **value** of an asset is an estimate of what that asset is economically worth to a particular investor.

Value may depend on:

- expectations;
    
- preferences;
    
- risk;
    
- future opportunities;
    
- strategic considerations;
    
- information;
    
- personal circumstances.
    

Therefore:

$$\boxed{\text{Price} \neq \text{Value}}$$

Different investors may assign very different values to exactly the same asset.

---

# 7. Example: The Bottle of Water

The bottle-of-water example was used again.

Suppose a bottle of water has a market price.

Its **value**, however, may be extremely different depending on circumstances.

If you:

- already have unlimited free water;
    
- are not thirsty;
    

you may assign almost no value to another bottle.

But if you:

- desperately need water;
    
- have no alternative source;
    

the same bottle may have very high value to you.

The market price may be identical.

The subjective value is not.

---

# 8. Strategic Value

This distinction is particularly important in corporate finance.

An asset may be much more valuable to one company than to another.

For example, a technology platform, brand, database, software company, or communication network may have limited standalone revenues but enormous strategic value to a particular acquirer.

This is one reason why acquisition prices cannot always be understood by looking only at current profits.

The buyer may value:

- users;
    
- technology;
    
- intellectual property;
    
- distribution;
    
- data;
    
- network effects;
    
- competitive positioning;
    
- strategic control.
    

This will become important when we eventually study valuation and M&A.

---

# 9. Review: Arbitrage

Another important review question was:

> What happens if exactly the same asset is simultaneously available at two different prices?

Suppose:

$$P_A < P_B$$

for two economically identical assets.

An investor could:

1. buy at $P_A$;
    
2. sell at $P_B$;
    
3. capture the difference.
    

This is:

## Arbitrage

---

# 10. No-Arbitrage Principle

Financial theory frequently assumes:

> **Persistent arbitrage opportunities should not exist in an efficient market.**

Why?

Because market participants will exploit them.

Their trades tend to eliminate the pricing inconsistency.

Therefore:

$$\boxed{\text{Identical Cash Flows} \Rightarrow \text{Identical Prices}}$$

This is closely related to the:

## Law of One Price

---

# 11. Market Efficiency

The session linked no-arbitrage reasoning with market efficiency.

At an introductory level:

> **Efficient markets should not contain obvious, persistent opportunities to obtain something for less than an economically identical alternative.**

A more rigorous discussion of market efficiency will come later.

For now, remember the intuition:

$$\text{Price inconsistency} \rightarrow \text{arbitrage} \rightarrow \text{trading} \rightarrow \text{price adjustment}$$

# 12. Introduction to Financial Markets

The session then shifted toward the actual structure of financial markets.

One useful distinction is between:

- the **primary market**;
    
- the **secondary market**.
    

Understanding this distinction is essential.

---

# 13. Primary Market

The **primary market** is the market in which a financial security is issued for the first time.

In a primary-market transaction:

> **Money goes from investors to the issuer.**

Examples:

- a company issues new bonds;
    
- a government issues Treasury securities;
    
- a company sells shares to investors through an IPO.
    

The issuer receives the financing.

---

# 14. A Simple Bond-Issuance Example

Suppose a company needs money today.

It creates a contract promising an investor:

### Year 1

\$5

### Year 2

\$5 + \$100

The investor may be willing to pay, for example:

\$90

today in exchange for those promised payments.

The company receives:

\$90

today.

The investor receives a contractual claim to the future payments.

This initial transaction takes place in the:

$$\boxed{\text{Primary Market}}$$

---

# 15. Secondary Market

Suppose the original investor later needs money before the bond reaches maturity.

The investor can sell the bond to another investor.

The company does **not** receive new financing from this transaction.

Instead:

- one investor sells;
    
- another investor buys.
    

This transaction takes place in the:

$$\boxed{\text{Secondary Market}}$$

---

# 16. Primary Versus Secondary Market

The distinction can be summarized as follows:

|Characteristic|Primary Market|Secondary Market|
|---|---|---|
|Security|Newly issued|Already existing|
|Seller|Issuer|Existing investor|
|Buyer|Investor|Another investor|
|Who receives the money?|Issuer|Selling investor|
|Main purpose|Raise capital|Provide liquidity|

This distinction applies to both:

- debt;
    
- equity.
    

---

# 17. IPO — Primary Market for Stocks

For stocks, one of the clearest examples of a primary-market transaction is:

## IPO — Initial Public Offering

An IPO occurs when a company sells shares to public investors as part of becoming publicly traded.

The IPO process normally involves:

- the company;
    
- investment banks;
    
- regulators;
    
- institutional investors;
    
- other potential shareholders.
    

Investment banks may help the company:

- prepare the offering;
    
- determine an appropriate valuation range;
    
- find investors;
    
- structure the transaction;
    
- establish an initial offering price.
    

Once shares are publicly traded, subsequent transactions normally take place in the:

$$\boxed{\text{Secondary Market}}$$

---

# 18. Stock Exchanges and the Secondary Market

When an investor buys shares of an already listed company, the investor normally buys them from another investor.

The company itself usually does not receive the money from that particular transaction.

Therefore:

> **Most everyday stock-market transactions are secondary-market transactions.**

This is an important exam-level distinction.

---

# 19. Why Secondary Markets Matter

A well-functioning secondary market provides:

## Liquidity

Investors know that they can potentially sell their securities before maturity.

That makes investors more willing to participate in primary-market financing.

Therefore:

$$\text{Liquid Secondary Market} \rightarrow \text{More Attractive Securities} \rightarrow \text{Easier Financing}$$

Primary and secondary markets are therefore deeply connected.

# 24. Real Assets and Financial Assets

The central theoretical topic of the session was the distinction between:

$$\boxed{\text{Real Assets}}$$

and:

$$\boxed{\text{Financial Assets}}$$

---

# 25. Real Assets

A **real asset** contributes directly to the productive capacity of an economy or has intrinsic economic usefulness.

Examples include:

- land;
    
- buildings;
    
- machinery;
    
- factories;
    
- equipment.
    

Real assets may be:

- tangible;
    
- intangible.
    

---

# 26. Tangible Real Assets

Tangible assets can physically be touched.

Examples:

- land;
    
- buildings;
    
- machines;
    
- factories;
    
- vehicles;
    
- equipment.
    

These assets can contribute directly to production or economic activity.

---

# 27. Intangible Real Assets

Real economic value can also come from assets that cannot physically be touched.

Examples include:

- patents;
    
- software;
    
- copyrights;
    
- brands;
    
- intellectual property;
    
- proprietary technology.
    

A song, for example, may generate royalties for decades even though the economically valuable asset is an intangible right.

---

# 28. Intellectual Property

The discussion of intellectual property illustrated an important principle:

> A large part of modern economic value is intangible.

Companies may derive enormous value from:

- software;
    
- algorithms;
    
- brands;
    
- trademarks;
    
- patents;
    
- copyrights;
    
- data;
    
- networks.
    

This becomes increasingly important in technology-driven economies.

---

# 29. Financial Assets

A **financial asset** is fundamentally different.

Its value comes from:

> **A contractual or ownership claim on future economic benefits.**

Examples include:

- bonds;
    
- stocks;
    
- bank deposits;
    
- loans;
    
- derivatives.
    

Unlike a factory or a piece of land, the financial asset derives its value from the rights that it gives to its owner.

---

# 30. Contractual Claims

Suppose somebody gives you a document saying:

> “I owe you \$100 and will repay you in one year.”

The paper itself has almost no physical value.

But the **contractual claim** may have substantial economic value.

That is the intuition behind many financial instruments.

---

# 31. The IOU Analogy

The expression:

## IOU

comes from:

> **“I owe you.”**

An IOU is not technically the same thing as a bond, but it provides a useful intuition.

Both involve a promise or obligation to make a future payment.

A bond formalizes this kind of financial relationship.

---

# 32. Financial Asset Versus Financial Liability

An important relationship is:

> **A financial asset for one party is usually a financial liability or equity claim for another party.**

For example:

If a company issues a bond:

### Investor

Owns a:

$$\text{Financial Asset}$$

### Company

Has a:

$$\text{Financial Liability}$$

The same contract appears on opposite sides of two economic relationships.

# 33. Financial Instrument — Formal Definition

The class briefly referred to the accounting definition of a financial instrument.

A standard definition is:

> A financial instrument is a contract that gives rise to a financial asset of one entity and a financial liability or equity instrument of another entity.

The key word is:

$$\boxed{\text{Contract}}$$

The value of a financial instrument ultimately comes from the economic rights and obligations contained in that contract.

---

# 34. Why Financial Assets Exist

Financial assets perform several extremely important economic functions.

Among them:

1. Transfer capital from savers to borrowers.
    
2. Allow companies and governments to finance investment.
    
3. Allow households to save.
    
4. Transfer and manage risk.
    
5. Allow consumption to be moved across time.
    
6. Improve the allocation of economic resources.
    
7. Provide liquidity.
    
8. Help markets establish prices.
    

---

# 35. Savers and Borrowers

Suppose one person has:

$$\text{Savings}$$

and another economic agent has:

$$\text{Investment Opportunities}$$

Financial markets connect them.

For example:

$$\text{Household Savings} \rightarrow \text{Financial Market} \rightarrow \text{Corporate Investment}$$

Without financial markets, many productive projects would never obtain financing.

---

# 36. Consumption Smoothing

Financial instruments also allow people to move purchasing power through time.

For example:

### Saving

Consume less today in order to consume more later.

### Borrowing

Consume or invest more today and repay later.

This is known as:

## Consumption Smoothing

Finance therefore connects:

$$\text{Today}$$

with:

$$\text{Tomorrow}$$

Another party may be willing to accept that risk in exchange for compensation.

# 53. Introduction to Bond Valuation

The most important quantitative part of Session 2 involved the valuation of bonds.

The fundamental idea is:

> **A bond is worth the present value of all the future payments promised to the bondholder.**

These payments may include:

1. coupons;
    
2. repayment of face value.
    

---

# 54. Anatomy of a Bond

Suppose a bond has:

### Face Value

$$F = \$1,000$$

### Coupon Rate

$$c = 8\%$$

### Maturity

$\$10\text{ years}$$

The face value is the amount the investor receives at maturity.

The coupon determines periodic interest payments.

---

# 55. Coupon

For an annual 8% coupon on a \$1,000 face-value bond:

$$C = 0.08(1000)$$

Therefore:

$$\boxed{C = \$80}$$

per year.

At maturity, the final payment would be:

\$80 + \$1,000

Therefore:

$$\boxed{\$1,080}$$

---

# 56. Bond Cash Flows

A simplified annual-coupon bond therefore generates:

|Year|Cash Flow|
|--:|--:|
|1|\$80|
|2|\$80|
|3|\$80|
|...|...|
|9|\$80|
|10|\$1,080|

These are the cash flows we need to value.

---

# 57. Pricing the Bond

From Session 1:

$$PV = \frac{FV}{(1+r)^t}$$

Therefore, every bond payment can be discounted separately.

For example:

$$PV(C_1) = \frac{80}{1+r} \qquad PV(C_2) = \frac{80}{(1+r)^2}$$

and so on.

The final payment is:

$$PV(C_{10}+F) = \frac{1080}{(1+r)^{10}}$$

---

# 58. General Bond-Pricing Formula

The price of a coupon-paying bond is:

$$\boxed{P = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{C_t}{(1+r)^t} + \frac{F}{(1+r)^T}}$$

If the coupon is constant:

$$\boxed{P = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{C}{(1+r)^t} + \frac{F}{(1+r)^T}}$$

Where:

- $P$ = bond price;
    
- $C$ = coupon payment;
    
- $F$ = face value;
    
- $r$ = required market return or discount rate;
    
- $T$ = maturity.
    

This is one of the fundamental formulas of fixed-income finance.

---

# 59. The Main Lesson

The instructor emphasized that this was the most important technical idea of Session 2:

> **The bond price is simply the sum of the present values of its future cash flows.**

In symbols:

$$\boxed{P_{\text{bond}} = PV(C_1) + PV(C_2) + \cdots + PV(C_T+F)}$$

The mechanics will soon become automatic through practice.

---

# 60. Coupon Rate Equal to Required Return

Suppose:

$$\text{Coupon Rate} = 8\%$$

and:

$$\text{Required Market Return} = 8\%$$

for a standard bond with matching payment conventions.

The bond should trade at approximately its face value:

$$\boxed{P = F}$$

A bond whose market price equals its face value is called:

## A Par Bond

---

# 61. Par Bond

Therefore:

$$\boxed{P = F \;\Rightarrow\; \text{Bond Trades at Par}}$$

For a \$1,000 face-value bond:

$$P = \$1,000$$

means that the bond is trading at par.

Later we will also study:

- discount bonds;
    
- premium bonds.
    

---

# 62. Why Payment Timing Matters

The session also compared bonds with different coupon-payment frequencies.

An important intuition is:

> **Receiving money earlier is better than receiving the same money later.**

Therefore the exact timing of coupons affects value.

A semiannual coupon bond distributes part of its cash flows earlier than an otherwise comparable annual-coupon bond.

The precise treatment of:

- nominal rates;
    
- effective rates;
    
- compounding frequency;
    

will be formalized later.

For Session 2, the key principle is simply:

$$\boxed{\text{Earlier Cash Flows} \Rightarrow \text{Higher Present Value}}$$

all else equal.

---

# 63. Bond Prices and Interest Rates

Session 1 introduced the relationship:

$$PV = \frac{FV}{(1+r)^t}$$

The same principle applies to bonds.

If the discount rate rises:

$$r\uparrow$$

the present value of the bond's future cash flows falls.

Therefore:

$$\boxed{\text{Interest Rates}\uparrow \;\Rightarrow\; \text{Bond Prices}\downarrow}$$

Conversely:

$$\boxed{\text{Interest Rates}\downarrow \;\Rightarrow\; \text{Bond Prices}\uparrow}$$

This is one of the most important relationships in the entire course.

---

# 64. Why This Happens

Suppose an existing bond pays:

8%

and market interest rates suddenly rise to:

10%

New investors can now obtain a higher return elsewhere.

The existing 8% bond becomes less attractive.

Its market price therefore has to fall until its effective return becomes competitive.

This is the economic intuition behind the inverse relationship.

---

# 65. Excel and Bond Valuation

Excel was used to calculate the present value of individual bond cash flows.

Conceptually, each cash flow follows:

```excel
=CashFlow/(1+Rate)^Time
```

For example:

```excel
=80/(1+$B\$2)^A5
```

where:

- `$B\$2` contains the discount rate;
    
- `A5` contains the timing of the cash flow.
    

Then the bond price can be calculated by summing all present values:

```excel
=SUM(PV_Range)
```

# 66. Absolute References Again

The **F4** shortcut from Session 1 becomes particularly useful here.

When copying the discounting formula across many cash flows, the interest-rate cell should remain fixed.

For example:

```excel
=$C5/(1+$B\$2)^A5
```

The:

```text

```

reference remains constant while the cash-flow and time cells change.

This makes bond valuation much faster.

\$100

# 71. Maturity and Required Return

The class introduced another intuition:

Would you rather receive:

\$100

in one year,

or:

\$100

- maturity;
    
- yield;
    
- interest-rate risk.
    

---

# 72. The Yield Curve

The discussion of Treasury securities provided a first informal introduction to the:

## Yield Curve

The yield curve compares yields on similar debt instruments with different maturities.

For example:

- 1-year Treasury yield;
    
- 2-year Treasury yield;
    
- 10-year Treasury yield;
    
- 30-year Treasury yield.
    

Under many conditions:

$$\text{Longer Maturity} \Rightarrow \text{Higher Yield}$$

but this is not always true.

---

# 73. Yield-Curve Inversion

Sometimes shorter-term rates are higher than longer-term rates.

This is known as an:

## Inverted Yield Curve

An inverted yield curve may reflect expectations that:

- economic growth will weaken;
    
- inflation will fall;
    
- central banks will cut interest rates in the future.
    

This will be studied later in the course.

For Session 2, students only need to recognize the concept.

---

# 74. Sovereign Credit Risk

The class also compared bonds issued by different governments.

Investors may consider one government more creditworthy than another.

If investors perceive greater risk, they generally demand:

$$\boxed{\text{Higher Required Return}}$$

This is another application of:

$$\boxed{\text{Higher Risk} \Rightarrow \text{Higher Required Return}}$$

---

# 75. Sovereign Spread

Within the euro area, countries use the same currency but issue their own government debt.

A comparison can therefore be made between the yield on:

- German government bonds;
    
- Spanish government bonds.
    

The difference is often interpreted as a measure of relative perceived sovereign risk.

In Spain this is commonly referred to as:

## Risk Premium / Sovereign Spread

Conceptually:

$$\boxed{\text{Spread} = Y_{\text{Spain}} - Y_{\text{Germany}}}$$

for comparable maturities.

---

# 76. Municipal Bonds

The session briefly introduced:

## Municipal Bonds

Municipal bonds are debt securities issued by:

- states;
    
- cities;
    
- municipalities;
    
- other public-sector entities.
    

One important characteristic in the United States is their potential tax treatment.

---

# 77. Taxes and Investment Returns

Suppose:

### Municipal Bond

Yield:

4%

### Taxable Treasury or Other Bond

Yield:

5%

If there were no taxes, investors would generally prefer:

5%

But taxes may change the comparison.

---

# 78. After-Tax Return

Suppose the taxable bond yields:

5%

and the investor's relevant tax rate is:

20%

The after-tax return becomes:

$\$5\%(1 - 0.20)$$

Therefore:

$$\boxed{4\%}$$

Now the investor compares:

$\$4\%\text{ tax-exempt}$$

with:

$\$4\%\text{ after tax}$$

The two investments may become economically equivalent.

---

# 79. Taxes Matter

This provides an important general lesson:

> **Investors should care about returns after taxes, not simply quoted returns before taxes.**

Taxes influence:

- investment choices;
    
- corporate finance;
    
- capital structure;
    
- valuation;
    
- portfolio decisions.
    

Taxes will appear again later in the course.

---

# 80. Corporate Bonds

Companies also borrow by issuing:

## Corporate Bonds

Corporate bonds are an extremely important part of fixed-income markets.

Unlike U.S. Treasury securities, corporate bonds involve:

## Credit Risk

---

# 81. Credit Risk

Credit risk is the risk that the borrower may fail to make the promised payments.

This may involve:

- missing interest payments;
    
- restructuring debt;
    
- failing to repay principal;
    
- bankruptcy.
    

Credit risk therefore affects the return investors demand.

---

# 82. Credit Ratings

The class introduced bond ratings as a measure of credit quality.

Major rating agencies include:

- Standard & Poor's;
    
- Moody's;
    
- Fitch.
    

Ratings attempt to summarize the creditworthiness of an issuer or security.

---

# 83. AAA and Credit Quality

At the high end of the rating scale:

## AAA

represents extremely high credit quality.

As credit quality deteriorates, ratings become lower.

The lower the rating:

$$\boxed{\text{Higher Credit Risk}}$$

normally implies:

$$\boxed{\text{Higher Required Yield}}$$

---

# 84. Risk and Financing Cost

Suppose Company A is considered extremely safe.

Suppose Company B is much more likely to default.

Investors will not lend to both companies at the same interest rate.

Company B must normally offer a higher return.

Therefore:

$$\boxed{\text{Higher Risk} \Rightarrow \text{Higher Required Return} \Rightarrow \text{Higher Financing Cost}}$$

This relationship is fundamental in corporate finance.

---

# 85. Investment Grade

Bond ratings can be divided broadly into:

## Investment Grade

Higher-quality debt.

and:

## Non-Investment Grade / High-Yield

Riskier debt.

High-yield bonds are sometimes informally called:

## Junk Bonds

The boundary between investment-grade and non-investment-grade debt is economically important because many institutional investors face restrictions on the securities they can hold.

---

# 86. Priority in Bankruptcy

The session also introduced the concept of:

## Priority

If a company fails, not every investor has the same right to receive money.

Some claims are paid before others.

A simplified order might involve:

1. senior secured creditors;
    
2. senior unsecured creditors;
    
3. subordinated creditors;
    
4. preferred equity;
    
5. common equity.
    

The precise order depends on the legal and contractual structure.

---

# 87. Equity as a Residual Claim

Shareholders are owners of the company.

But in bankruptcy they normally receive money only **after creditors have been paid**.

This makes equity a:

## Residual Claim

In simplified form:

$$\boxed{\text{Equity Value} = \text{Value Remaining After Debt Claims}}$$

This helps explain why equity is generally riskier than senior debt.

---

# 88. Bondholders Versus Shareholders

A useful introductory comparison:

|Bondholder|Shareholder|
|---|---|
|Lender|Owner|
|Contractual payments|Residual claim|
|Coupon/interest|Potential dividends|
|Principal repayment|No maturity repayment|
|Higher priority|Lower priority|
|Usually lower risk|Usually higher risk|
|Usually lower expected return|Usually higher expected return|

This relationship will be developed further later.

## Commercial Paper

Commercial paper is short-term corporate debt.

Conceptually, it resembles a formal short-term promise to repay money.

The IOU analogy can therefore again be useful.

---

# 91. Futures

A futures contract creates an obligation involving the purchase or sale of an underlying asset at a future date under standardized terms.

---

# 92. Forwards

A forward contract also involves agreeing today on a transaction that will occur in the future.

Unlike futures, forwards are normally privately negotiated and commonly associated with OTC markets.

---

# 93. Options

An option gives its holder a **right**, but not necessarily an obligation, to buy or sell an underlying asset under specified conditions.

The distinction between:

- obligation;
    
- right;
    

will become very important.

---

# 94. Swaps

A swap is a contract in which parties agree to exchange future cash flows according to predetermined rules.

Common examples include:

- interest-rate swaps;
    
- currency swaps.
    

These will be studied later.

# 95. Securitization

The final instrument discussed briefly was securitization.

Suppose a bank owns thousands of mortgages.

Instead of holding each mortgage individually, the bank may pool them.

Conceptually:

$$\text{Mortgage 1} + \text{Mortgage 2} + \cdots + \text{Mortgage }N$$

can be transformed into securities backed by the pool.

This creates:

## Mortgage-Backed Securities

Securitization played an important role in the 2008 financial crisis.
